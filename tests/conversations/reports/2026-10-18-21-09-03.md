## Top problems

1. **Lines are too long and too idiomatic for A1–A2.** Only 0–29% of lines are followable against the 90% goal, and median replies are 16–38 words against about 10 for real turns. Example: "Grazie a lei, giusto giusto, mi piacciono i conti che tornano!" Davide (86% of replies over 30 words) and Francesca (longest reply 47 words) are the worst for length.
2. **Characters don't help in English when a lost learner asks.** Dario answered an English question only in Italian: "Ho detto che la pizza è croccante fuori e morbida dentro." Roberto did the same for "ricetta": "La ricetta è come si prepara un dolce…". Dario's "slower" repeat was barely simpler than the original.
3. **Characters comment on the learner's Italian or turn into teachers.** Examples: "Bravo, parla benissimo!", "Il suo italiano va benissimo.", and Francesca's muddled "farà come gli uccellini, correrà veloce! Hai fatto una bella domanda." This is minor but may be why "alive" sits at 5–6.

## Recommendations

1. **Tell the model who it's talking to, in `build-turn-prompt.ts`.** Add one situational line per turn, such as: "The person in front of you is just starting Italian. In a real exchange like this, you say a short thing and then wait for them." Pass the learner's level in that line. This describes the situation without adding a length rule to `character.md`. It should shorten replies and make idioms like "giusto giusto" less likely. Re-run these six conversations and check that median reply length approaches 10 words and "beginner can follow" rises.

2. **Add one line to `character.md` about what a real person would do for a lost foreigner.** For example: "They're a visitor who is learning Italian. If they're lost or ask in English what something means, you'd happily give them the word in English, and if you repeat something, you'd say it in simpler words." This covers both the missing English help and the "slower" repeat that wasn't simpler. It describes the situation rather than imposing a rule, and it fixes the most damaging failure, a stuck A1 learner who gets no help.

3. **Hold off on the praise and teacher-voice problem.** It's minor. If recommendation 1 shortens replies, the unprompted level comments and Francesca's flourish will likely shrink with it. If they persist after that change, add "you're a baker, not a teacher" to the character framing. Optionally, also set the address form (lei or tu) in each character's data so Filippo doesn't mix them.

## How this test works

1. **Sample.** Random characters from the city files (one per city), each at their own place.
2. **Simulate.** Claude plays a learner for 6 turns. The character's replies come from `lib/turn.ts`, the same code the app uses, so this is the real prompt.
3. **Grade.** A separate Claude call reads each transcript and scores the character 1–10 (10 = a native speaker would find it entirely natural):
   - **language**: Speaks Italian. Uses English when the learner asks for help (in English or in Italian), as much as the help takes, then returns to Italian; no English nobody asked for.
   - **understands**: Understands imperfect speech. Reads garbled speech-to-text and learner errors as what they meant, without commenting on them.
   - **adapts**: Adapts when the learner is lost: when the learner signals confusion the character slows down and rephrases, and when they ask to repeat, repeats.
   - **alive**: Feels like a real person in that place. Scored by the judge that scores real conversations (quality.md), not by this grader.
4. **Measure length.** Counted, not graded: words in the opener, median and longest reply, share of replies over 30 words, questions per reply, and share asking more than one question (several threads at once). Yardstick: real conversational turns run about 1–2 seconds, roughly 5–10 words (Heldner & Edlund 2015; Stivers et al. 2009). These are measurements only; nothing is sent to the characters.
5. **Recommend.** One more call reads all the grades and lengths and suggests up to 3 changes. Nothing is changed automatically.

**Learner types**

- **english-beginner**: An American beginner (A1). Short, simple sentences. Typical mistakes: "voglio" instead of "vorrei", wrong article gender ("il pizza"), English word order. Some lines arrive garbled by speech-to-text, e.g. "bone journal" for "buongiorno", "quando costa" for "quanto costa", "gratsie" for "grazie".
- **hesitant**: A nervous beginner (A1). Hesitates ("ehm... io... "), sometimes says "ripeti per favore" or "più lentamente", and once or twice gives up and asks in English ("sorry, what?" or "do you speak English?").
- **improving**: A learner around A2 who has been in Italy a few weeks. Uses past tense and longer sentences with occasional errors ("sono andato al mercato ieri e ho comprato le pomodori"). Curious and chatty; asks the character about themselves and the neighbourhood.
- All learners are told to be honest: when a line is too fast or complex for their level, they say so instead of pretending to follow.

**Reading the results**

- The sample is small and the conversations are random, so expect grades to move by about half a point between runs with no changes. Look for problems that repeat, not single scores.
- The learner is simulated. It catches clear failures (switching to English, correcting speech-to-text noise, never recovering when the learner is lost) but is no substitute for talking to the characters yourself.
- To compare before and after a change, run both with the same `--seed` (same characters and learners) and compare the grades and length numbers. The learner's lines still vary between runs, so look at clear differences, not small ones.
