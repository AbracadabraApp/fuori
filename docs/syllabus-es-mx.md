# Syllabus: Mexican Spanish, A1–C1

2026-10-22 · Josh · Magda's model syllabus for Afuera (Mexico) (`docs/teach-plan.md`). The data is `content/es-mx/syllabus.json` (units, grammar inventory) and `content/es-mx/syllabus-words.csv` (word list); this page is the readable version for review. About the language and real-life situations only, not the game's places or mechanics.

**How it was made:** every source was read in full, one reader per book and six for the PCIC (notes on Josh's laptop in `books-private/syllabus-working/magda-mx/`); the sources were lined up and the units ordered for a newcomer's first weeks at A1 and by the books' and the PCIC's progression above, with every Spain-only form and level placement adapted to Mexico; each level was written in full; a second teacher reviewed every Spanish line and its English and fixed what was wrong. The course books stop at A2, so B1–C1 rest on the PCIC (adapted), Seven Keys and Word Power: a native Mexican review matters most there.

**Size:** A1 15 units, A2 15 units, B1 13 units, B2 13 units, C1 13 units; 610 key phrases; 3300 words in the word list; grammar inventory of 348 points.

**Each unit has:** what you can do after it, situations, key phrases (each to *say* or only to *understand*), 2–3 model exchanges, the grammar the phrases need with the trap for English speakers, vocabulary across parts of speech (with flashcard image kind), pronunciation, common mistakes, one culture point, what it recycles, the evidence that you have it, and when to review it. The full detail is in the JSON; below are the essentials.


## A1: the first weeks

### a1-01 · Greet and take leave

**You can:** Greet people and say goodbye at any time of day, with usted by default and tú with people your age who use it first.  
**Situations:** walking into a shop or a waiting room and greeting everyone, meeting a neighbour by the door, greeting an older person or an official, leaving a shop  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *¡Buenos días!* | Good morning! | say |
| *¡Buenas tardes! / ¡Buenas noches!* | Good afternoon! / Good evening! | say |
| *Buenas.* | Hi. | say |
| *Buenos días, señora. ¿Cómo está?* | Good morning, ma'am. How are you? | say |
| *Muy bien, gracias. ¿Y usted?* | Very well, thanks. And you? | say |
| *¿Qué tal? / ¿Cómo estás?* | How's it going? / How are you? | say |
| *Bien, gracias. ¿Y tú?* | Fine, thanks. And you? | say |
| *Hasta luego. Que le vaya bien.* | Bye. Have a good day. | say |
| *Gracias, igualmente.* | Thanks, same to you. | say |
| *¡Nos vemos! / ¡Hasta mañana!* | See you! / See you tomorrow! | say |

**Grammar:** usted and tú as two sets of chunks; estar for how you are; Greeting by time of day; ustedes for any group; que le vaya bien as a fixed goodbye  
**Words:** 25 (buenos días, buenas tardes, buenas noches, buenas, ¿qué tal?, estar, bien, mal, más o menos, gracias, …)  
**Culture:** Mexicans greet when they walk into a small shop, a waiting room or a lift, and say goodbye when they leave; a quick 'buenas' is enough, and silence comes across as cold. When you meet someone, men usually shake hands; in social settings a man and a woman, or two women, usually greet with one light kiss on the right cheek, often from the first meeting, while at work and in offices a handshake is the safe choice. Using usted is a sign of respect, not distance.  
**Evidence:** Without being prompted, the learner greets on arrival with the right greeting for the time of day, answers ¿cómo está? with (muy) bien, gracias and returns the question in the same register (usted with an older or unfamiliar character, tú with a peer who uses tú first), and leaves with que le vaya bien or nos vemos.

### a1-02 · When you don't understand

**You can:** Say you didn't understand, ask for a repeat, a slower pace, a spelling or a word, and say how much Spanish you speak.  
**Situations:** someone speaks fast at a counter, spelling your name for a form or an order, an unknown word on a sign, a neighbour uses a word you don't know  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *¿Mande?* | Sorry? / Pardon? | say |
| *Perdón, no entendí.* | Sorry, I didn't understand. | say |
| *¿Me lo puede repetir, por favor?* | Could you say that again, please? | say |
| *Más despacio, por favor.* | More slowly, please. | say |
| *¿Qué significa «banqueta»?* | What does 'banqueta' mean? | say |
| *¿Cómo se dice «receipt» en español?* | How do you say 'receipt' in Spanish? | say |
| *¿Me lo puede escribir?* | Could you write it down for me? | say |
| *Hablo un poquito de español. Estoy aprendiendo.* | I speak a little Spanish. I'm learning. | say |
| *¿Cómo se escribe? ¿Con be grande o con ve chica?* | How do you spell it? With a B or a V? | say |
| *¿Me deletrea su apellido, por favor?* | Could you spell your surname, please? | understand |

**Grammar:** Repair chunks come first; ¿Mande? for 'pardon?'; lo = 'it' inside chunks; poder + infinitive for polite requests; Spelling aloud with Mexican letter names  
**Words:** 21 (¿mande?, perdón, entender, repetir, despacio, significar, decir, escribir, deletrear, poder, …)  
**Culture:** Asking a Mexican '¿me entendiste?' can feel like a test of their intelligence, so people rephrase rather than check. The other side of this is that you, as a newcomer, are expected to ask: ¿mande?, ¿me lo repite? and ¿qué significa…? are normal and well received, and most people slow down and rephrase gladly. Mexicans are generous with foreigners who try to speak Spanish.  
**Evidence:** When the learner misses something, they ask for repair in Spanish (¿mande?, no entendí, ¿me lo puede repetir?, más despacio) instead of switching to English or going silent; they ask what a word means or how to say one, and can spell their name with Spanish letter names.

### a1-03 · Introduce yourself as a newcomer

**You can:** Say your name, where you're from, where you live now and how long you've been here, and swap numbers.  
**Situations:** meeting a neighbour for the first time, being introduced at a gathering, giving details at a counter  
**After:** a1-01  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *Me llamo … ¿Y usted?* | My name is … And you? | say |
| *¿Cómo se llama? / ¿Cómo te llamas?* | What's your name? (usted / tú) | say |
| *Mucho gusto. — Igualmente.* | Nice to meet you. — Likewise. | say |
| *Soy de …, pero ahora vivo aquí.* | I'm from …, but I live here now. | say |
| *¿De dónde es usted? / ¿De dónde eres?* | Where are you from? (usted / tú) | say |
| *Vivo en la colonia …* | I live in the … neighbourhood. | say |
| *Llevo dos semanas en México. / Tengo dos semanas aquí.* | I've been in Mexico for two weeks. / I've been here two weeks. | say |
| *¿Cuánto tiempo lleva aquí?* | How long have you been here? | understand |
| *¿Tienes WhatsApp? ¿Me pasas tu número?* | Are you on WhatsApp? Can I have your number? | say |

**Grammar:** llamarse as a chunk; ser de for where you're from; vivir en: present tense for yo, tú, usted; llevar / tener + time for how long; Question words  
**Words:** 24 (llamarse, ser, vivir, llevar, mucho gusto, encantado, nombre, apellido, colonia, calle, …)  
**Culture:** Mexicans usually ask early on how long you've been here and whether you like it, and are often delighted that you're learning Spanish. Numbers are swapped for WhatsApp, which almost everyone uses, from neighbours to plumbers. Every address includes the colonia (and a postcode, código postal), which matters more than the street name for finding a place.  
**Evidence:** Meeting a character for the first time, the learner gives their name with me llamo, says where they're from with soy de and that they live here now, says how long they've been here with llevo / tengo + time, asks the same back in the right register, and answers mucho gusto with igualmente.

### a1-04 · Everyday courtesy

**You can:** Use the small words Mexicans expect: ask to pass, excuse yourself, thank and answer thanks, wish people a good meal, address people politely.  
**Situations:** squeezing past people on a bus or in a queue, leaving a table where others are still eating, being greeted by a shopkeeper, asking a stranger for help  
**After:** a1-01  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *Con permiso.* | Excuse me. | say |
| *Disculpe, ¿me ayuda con algo?* | Excuse me, could you help me with something? | say |
| *Perdón. — No pasa nada.* | Sorry. — It's fine. | say |
| *¡Provecho!* | Enjoy your meal! | say |
| *Gracias. — De nada. / Con mucho gusto.* | Thanks. — You're welcome. / My pleasure. | say |
| *A sus órdenes.* | At your service. | understand |
| *¡Pásele, joven! ¿Qué buscaba?* | Come in! What were you looking for? | understand |
| *Nada más estoy viendo, gracias.* | I'm just looking, thanks. | say |
| *Un momentito, por favor.* | Just a moment, please. | say |
| *Buenos días, don Pepe. / Buenas tardes, doña Lupita.* | Good morning, don Pepe. / Good afternoon, doña Lupita. | say |

**Grammar:** Courtesy chunks, no analysis; disculpe / disculpa; The diminutive -ito as a softener; Forms of address; The polite past, heard only  
**Words:** 20 (con permiso, disculpe, perdón, provecho, de nada, con mucho gusto, a sus órdenes, por favor, muy amable, no pasa nada, …)  
**Culture:** Mexican courtesy is constant and small: con permiso when you pass someone or leave a table, provecho to anyone eating as you walk by (even strangers in a restaurant), and a greeting for the whole room. Shop and market staff call out ¡pásele! and address you as joven, señorita or güero/güera; staff and officials answer thanks with a sus órdenes or con mucho gusto. Getting these small words right earns more goodwill than perfect grammar.  
**Evidence:** The learner uses con permiso to pass or leave, provecho to people eating, disculpe to get a stranger's attention and perdón after a slip, thanks people and answers thanks with de nada or con mucho gusto, and addresses people with señor, señora, joven or don/doña + first name.

### a1-05 · Order food and drink

**You can:** Order at a taquería, a food stand or a café, say how you want it, and ask what's in it.  
**Situations:** a taco stand at lunchtime, a café counter, a stand at the market's eating area  
**After:** a1-04  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *¿Qué va a querer? / ¿Qué le damos?* | What would you like? | understand |
| *Me da tres tacos de pastor, por favor.* | Can I have three al pastor tacos, please? | say |
| *¿Para aquí o para llevar?* | For here or to go? | understand |
| *Para aquí. / Para llevar, por favor.* | For here. / To go, please. | say |
| *¿Con todo? — Sí, con todo. / Sin cebolla, por favor.* | With everything? — Yes, with everything. / No onion, please. | say |
| *¿Pica?* | Is it spicy? | say |
| *¿Qué lleva la torta?* | What's in the torta? | say |
| *Un agua de horchata y un café de olla, por favor.* | An horchata and a café de olla, please. | say |
| *¿Me regala una servilleta?* | Could I have a napkin? | say |

**Grammar:** ¿Me da …? to order; un / una and plurals; de for fillings and flavours; con / sin; ¿Me regala …? for small things  
**Words:** 22 (taco, torta, quesadilla, al pastor, salsa, cebolla, cilantro, limón, café de olla, agua, …)  
**Culture:** At a taco stand you usually eat standing, pay at the end and tell the taquero how many you had; he keeps count too. Con todo means onion and cilantro; salsas and limes are on the counter, and limón in Mexico is the small green lime. Don't drink tap water: ask for agua natural (still), agua mineral (sparkling) or an agua fresca such as horchata or jamaica.  
**Evidence:** At a food stand or café the learner orders with me da … por favor, says con todo or sin + ingredient, asks ¿pica? or ¿qué lleva?, answers ¿para aquí o para llevar?, and asks for small things politely with ¿me da …? or ¿me regala …?.

### a1-06 · Numbers, prices and paying

**You can:** Understand prices, ask how much, pay in cash or by card, sort out change, and give a phone number.  
**Situations:** paying at a small shop that has no change, a card terminal brought to the table, giving your number to a neighbour  
**After:** a1-05  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *¿Cuánto es? / ¿Cuánto le debo?* | How much is it? / What do I owe you? | say |
| *Son ochenta y cinco pesos.* | That's 85 pesos. | understand |
| *¿Aceptan tarjeta?* | Do you take cards? | say |
| *Nada más efectivo.* | Cash only. | understand |
| *¿Con tarjeta o en efectivo?* | Card or cash? | understand |
| *¿Tiene cambio de quinientos?* | Do you have change for a 500? | say |
| *No traigo cambio, perdón.* | Sorry, I don't have any change on me. | say |
| *¿Me cobra, por favor?* | Can I pay, please? | say |
| *Aquí tiene su cambio.* | Here's your change. | understand |
| *Mi celular es cincuenta y cinco, doce, treinta y cuatro …* | My mobile number is 55 12 34 … | say |

**Grammar:** Numbers 0–100; Hundreds and thousands for prices; ser for totals, costar for prices; traer = to have on you; Prices and phone numbers, Mexican style  
**Words:** 22 (peso, centavo, billete, moneda, cambio, efectivo, tarjeta, terminal, cajero, cuenta, …)  
**Culture:** Cash is still everyday money at markets, stalls, small shops and peseros, and change is often scarce: break 500 and 1,000 notes at supermarkets, convenience stores or the bank, and keep coins and 20s and 50s for small purchases. In restaurants the waiter brings the card terminal to the table, so your card doesn't leave your sight. A tip of about 10–15% is normal in sit-down restaurants.  
**Evidence:** The learner asks ¿cuánto es? or ¿cuánto le debo?, understands a spoken price in the hundreds, asks ¿aceptan tarjeta?, deals with a change problem (¿tiene cambio de …?, no traigo cambio), asks for the bill with ¿me cobra?, and gives a ten-digit number in pairs.

### a1-07 · Ask whether they have things

**You can:** Ask for things in a small shop, point to what you want, and close the purchase.  
**Situations:** the corner shop, a bakery with a tray and tongs, a stationer's for copies  
**After:** a1-06  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *¿Tiene pilas?* | Do you have batteries? | say |
| *¿Hay pan dulce?* | Is there any pan dulce? | say |
| *¿Me da ese, por favor? El de allá.* | Can I have that one, please? The one over there. | say |
| *¿Cuánto cuesta este?* | How much is this one? | say |
| *¿Algo más? — No, es todo, gracias.* | Anything else? — No, that's all, thanks. | say |
| *Ya no hay. / Ya se acabó.* | There's none left. / It's sold out. | understand |
| *Nomás eso.* | Just that. | say |
| *Me lo llevo.* | I'll take it. | say |

**Grammar:** tener vs hay; este / ese / aquel with acá / allá; nomás = just, only; ya no = no longer, none left; me lo llevo as a chunk  
**Words:** 25 (tienda, panadería, papelería, pila, pan dulce, concha, bolillo, leche, huevo, charola, …)  
**Culture:** The tiendita on the corner sells a bit of everything and often gets to know its regulars. In most bakeries you serve yourself: take a tray (charola) and tongs (pinzas), choose your bread, and take it to the till to be bagged and charged. Papelerías do copies, printing and scans, which you'll need for any paperwork.  
**Evidence:** The learner asks a shop whether they have something (¿tiene …?) or whether there is any (¿hay …?), points with este / ese / el de allá when the word is missing, understands ya no hay, answers ¿algo más? with es todo or nomás eso, and closes with me lo / la llevo.

### a1-08 · At the market

**You can:** Buy fruit, vegetables and tortillas by weight, ask the price per kilo, taste before buying, and follow the stallholder's patter.  
**Situations:** a market stall, a tortillería, a weekly street market (tianguis)  
**After:** a1-07  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *¿Qué le damos, güera?* | What can we get you? | understand |
| *¿A cómo está el kilo de jitomate?* | How much is a kilo of tomatoes? | say |
| *Deme medio kilo, por favor.* | Give me half a kilo, please. | say |
| *Un kilo de tortillas, por favor.* | A kilo of tortillas, please. | say |
| *¿Me da a probar?* | Can I try some? | say |
| *Están bien dulces, ándele.* | They're really sweet, go on. | understand |
| *Ahí le va su pilón.* | There's a little extra for you. | understand |
| *¿Quién sigue?* | Who's next? | understand |
| *¿Cuánto le doy?* | How much do I owe you? | say |

**Grammar:** Quantities + de; estar a for prices that change; deme, pruebe: usted commands as chunks; bien + adjective = really; Being addressed: güero, güera, marchante  
**Words:** 22 (mercado, tianguis, tortillería, tortilla, kilo, medio, cuarto, gramo, jitomate, tomate, …)  
**Culture:** Markets run on relationships: buy from the same stalls and you become a marchante, with better fruit picked for you and a pilón, a small free extra. Vendors are happy to let you taste (¿me da a probar?) and will ask whether you want avocados for today or for later. Bargaining is fine at a tianguis and at craft stalls, but not for everyday food at fixed-price stalls. Bring your own bag, and buy tortillas by the kilo, hot from the tortillería.  
**Evidence:** At a stall the learner asks ¿a cómo está el kilo de …?, orders by weight with medio kilo / un kilo / un cuarto de, asks to taste, understands the vendor's patter (¿qué le damos?, ¿algo más?, ahí le va su pilón, ¿quién sigue?) and pays.

### a1-09 · Asking the way

**You can:** Ask where something is and follow simple directions given in blocks, corners and landmarks.  
**Situations:** looking for an ATM, finding a stop or a station, asking a shopkeeper the way  
**After:** a1-04  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *Disculpe, ¿dónde está el metro?* | Excuse me, where's the metro? | say |
| *¿Hay un cajero por aquí?* | Is there an ATM around here? | say |
| *¿Dónde queda el mercado?* | Where's the market? | say |
| *¿Qué tan lejos está?* | How far is it? | say |
| *Está a dos cuadras.* | It's two blocks away. | understand |
| *Siga derecho y dé vuelta a la derecha.* | Go straight on and turn right. | understand |
| *Está enfrente de la farmacia, en la esquina.* | It's opposite the pharmacy, on the corner. | understand |
| *¿Derecho dos cuadras y a la derecha?* | Straight on two blocks and then right? | say |
| *Gracias, muy amable.* | Thanks, that's very kind. | say |

**Grammar:** estar for location, hay for whether one exists; a + distance; Directions as usted commands (understand); Place words; del and al  
**Words:** 24 (cuadra, esquina, banqueta, semáforo, glorieta, avenida, cajero, farmacia, iglesia, súper, …)  
**Culture:** Directions in Mexico are given in cuadras (blocks) and landmarks (the church, the pharmacy, the convenience store) more than street names. Repeating the directions back is normal and helps. Some people would rather give you directions than say they don't know, so if you're unsure, ask a second person. Thank with 'muy amable'.  
**Evidence:** The learner asks where a place is with ¿dónde está / queda …? or whether there's one nearby with ¿hay un … por aquí?, asks how far, follows directions given in cuadras and landmarks, repeats them back to check, and thanks the person.

### a1-10 · Times, days and opening hours

**You can:** Ask and tell the time, ask when somewhere opens, understand that "hasta" can mean "not until", and fix a time to meet.  
**Situations:** checking when a shop or an office opens, arranging to meet a neighbour, telling someone you'll be back  
**After:** a1-06  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *¿Qué horas son? — Son las cinco y media.* | What time is it? — It's half past five. | say |
| *Son veinte para las ocho. / Es cuarto para las tres.* | It's twenty to eight. / It's quarter to three. | understand |
| *¿A qué hora abren? / ¿A qué hora cierran?* | What time do you open? / What time do you close? | say |
| *Abren hasta las diez.* | They don't open until ten. | understand |
| *De lunes a viernes, de nueve a seis.* | Monday to Friday, nine to six. | understand |
| *Mañana en la mañana.* | Tomorrow morning. | say |
| *¿Nos vemos el sábado a las cinco? — ¡Sale!* | Shall we meet on Saturday at five? — Sure! | say |
| *Ahorita vengo.* | I'll be right back. | say |

**Grammar:** ser for the time: es la una, son las dos; a for 'at'; days with el; hasta = 'not until' in Mexico; de … a … for ranges; en la mañana / en la tarde / en la noche; The present for plans; saying yes; ahorita and al rato: elastic time  
**Words:** 21 (hora, minuto, y media, cuarto, en punto, lunes, fin de semana, enero, horario, abrir, …)  
**Culture:** Shops in Mexican cities generally stay open through the middle of the day, and convenience stores open long hours. Appointments with doctors, offices and banks start more or less on time, but social plans are looser: arriving a bit late to a party at someone's home is normal, and invitations rarely have an end time. Ahorita is famous for its elasticity; Mexicans themselves joke about it.  
**Evidence:** The learner asks and tells the time (¿qué horas son?, son las …), asks when a place opens and closes, understands hasta + time as 'not until', arranges a day and time with ¿nos vemos el … a las …?, and agrees with sale or va.

### a1-11 · Getting around

**You can:** Take buses, peseros, the metro and taxis: ask where a bus goes, ask the fare, get off where you want, and choose a safe taxi.  
**Situations:** catching a bus or pesero, the metro, getting a safe taxi at night  
**After:** a1-09, a1-10  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *¿Este camión pasa por el centro?* | Does this bus go through the centre? | say |
| *¿Cuánto es el pasaje?* | How much is the fare? | say |
| *¡Bajan! / En la esquina, por favor.* | Getting off! / At the corner, please. | say |
| *¿Qué línea tomo para ir al centro?* | Which line do I take to get to the centre? | say |
| *¿Dónde me bajo para ir al mercado?* | Where do I get off for the market? | say |
| *¿Me pide un taxi de sitio, por favor?* | Could you call me a taxi from the rank, please? | say |
| *Aquí está bien, gracias.* | Here's fine, thanks. | say |
| *Ya estoy afuera, en un carro gris.* | I'm outside, in a grey car. | understand |
| *Voy en metro. / Voy caminando.* | I'm going by metro. / I'm walking. | say |

**Grammar:** tomar for buses, the metro and taxis; ir en + vehicle; caminando; pasar por; subirse and bajarse; ir vs venir  
**Words:** 21 (camión, pesero, metrobús, taxi, taxi de sitio, aplicación, estación, línea, pasaje, tarjeta, …)  
**Culture:** Most cities run on camiones and peseros: you flag them anywhere on the route, pay the driver (often in coins), and call out ¡bajan! or 'en la esquina, por favor' to get off. Mexico City's metro is cheap and busy, with women-and-children-only carriages at the front, and the Metrobús is a fast bus with its own lanes and stations. At night, take a taxi de sitio (from a rank or called for you) or a ride app rather than hailing a taxi in the street.  
**Evidence:** The learner asks whether a bus goes through a place (¿pasa por …?), asks the fare, asks where to get off and says ¡bajan! or 'en la esquina, por favor', asks which metro line to take, asks for a taxi de sitio, and says how they get around with tomar / ir en / caminando.

### a1-12 · Your home

**You can:** Describe where you live, find your way round a flat, and deal with the basics: water, the landlord, the bathroom.  
**Situations:** showing someone your flat, the water delivery, telling the landlord something's missing  
**After:** a1-03, a1-09  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *Rento un departamento en la colonia …* | I rent a flat in the … neighbourhood. | say |
| *Está en el tercer piso.* | It's on the third floor. | say |
| *Tiene dos recámaras y una cocina chiquita.* | It has two bedrooms and a small kitchen. | say |
| *¿Dónde está el baño?* | Where's the bathroom? | say |
| *No hay agua caliente.* | There's no hot water. | say |
| *Mi casero vive abajo.* | My landlord lives downstairs. | say |
| *El agua de la llave no se toma.* | You don't drink the tap water. | understand |
| *¿Me deja dos garrafones, por favor?* | Could you leave me two jugs of water, please? | say |

**Grammar:** hay vs está in a home; Ordinals for floors; tener for what a home has; mi, tu, su; The diminutive for size and warmth  
**Words:** 24 (departamento, casa, recámara, sala, cocina, baño, regadera, ventana, azotea, piso, …)  
**Culture:** Don't drink tap water in Mexico: households buy garrafones of purified water, delivered by trucks or bicycles that call out in the street. Water is stored in a tinaco on the roof (and sometimes a cistern), and hot water comes from a gas water heater; if the gas runs out, so does the hot water. Gas comes in tanks delivered by trucks you'll hear in the street. Most homes have no central heating.  
**Evidence:** The learner describes their home with tiene + rooms, says which floor it's on, asks where something is with ¿dónde está …?, reports a problem with no hay …, and orders water or asks the landlord for help politely.

### a1-13 · Likes, food and the weather

**You can:** Say what you like, agree or disagree with someone's likes, and make small talk about the food and the weather.  
**Situations:** chatting while waiting in line, a neighbour's comment on the rain, talking about food with someone you just met  
**After:** a1-05  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *Me gusta mucho la comida de aquí.* | I really like the food here. | say |
| *Me encantan los tamales.* | I love tamales. | say |
| *A mí también. / A mí no. / A mí tampoco.* | Me too. / Not me. / Me neither. | say |
| *¿Te gusta México? — ¡Sí, mucho!* | Do you like Mexico? — Yes, a lot! | say |
| *¡Qué rico!* | How delicious! | say |
| *¡Hace mucho calor hoy!* | It's really hot today! | say |
| *Parece que va a llover, ¿verdad?* | Looks like it's going to rain, doesn't it? | say |

| *A quien prefiere todo lo extranjero le dicen malinchista.* | Someone who prefers everything foreign gets called a malinchista. | understand |

**Grammar:** historical narrative: preterite, passive, impersonal; relatives: quienes, cuyo, el cual; hasta donde sé; según  
**Words:** 20 (Conquista, Independencia, Reforma, Revolución, mestizaje, pueblos indígenas, náhuatl, temblor, sismo, simulacro, …)  
**Culture:** History is part of daily life in Mexico: streets are named after dates (5 de Mayo, 16 de Septiembre, 20 de Noviembre), 19 September brings the national earthquake drill and memories of 1985 and 2017, and every 2 October there's a march for the students killed in 1968. Knowing the basics earns real respect, and newcomers from the US especially should know about the war of 1846–48 and the Niños Héroes. Ask questions and let Mexicans lead any criticism of their own history.  
**Evidence:** In a conversation about the past or a commemoration, the learner tells events in the preterite and passive, uses a relative such as quienes or cuyo correctly, hedges with hasta donde sé or según, and raises a sensitive subject (migration, 1968, the earthquakes) with care.

### c1-13 · Community life and mediation

**You can:** Clear up misunderstandings, explain what someone meant, and help newcomers and Mexicans understand each other.  
**Situations:** a residents' meeting, helping a new arrival, smoothing over a misunderstanding, explaining a custom  
**After:** b2-07, c1-05  

| Spanish | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *Perdón, creo que hubo un malentendido.* | Sorry, I think there's been a misunderstanding. | say |
| *Lo que quiso decir es que no está de acuerdo con la cuota.* | What she meant is that she doesn't agree with the fee. | say |
| *Ella se refiere a que nadie le avisó de la junta.* | What she's getting at is that nobody told her about the meeting. | say |

**Still not covered by any unit.** These are smaller and can live inside units or in Learn's "good to know":
- driving: licence, *verificación*, *Hoy No Circula*, *casetas*, *topes*, fines

The pronunciation strand should be one or two points per unit, as `teach-plan.md` asks, rather than a unit of its own:
- A1: pure vowels, seseo, the tap r vs the trilled rr, stress (*hablo / habló*), question intonation
- A2–B1: soft b/d/g between vowels, linking words (*tomar algo*), *este …* as a filler
- B2: fast highland speech with weak vowels
- C1: Nahuatl *x* and *tl*, regional accents
