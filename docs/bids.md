# Conversation as bids

**Draft, 2026-11-12, for Josh's deep dive. Not agreed.** The model is Josh's. Lines marked *Claude* are additions for him to keep, change or drop. Josh's words are quoted exactly as he typed them, typos included. The examples are from saved visits. No outside research is used here (it's in `dialogue-structure-reference.md` § 1a).

## The model (Josh)

- "A conversation is all about bids… every turn is an opportunity to bid/accept."
- "Character makes a bid. Learner accepts and makes their bid. Character accepts, repeat."
- "if the bid stinks it doesn't get accepted."
- "I think bids can help evaluate - but they are also the first conceptual model that seems to hold up (it is very simple)."
- "bids might be a framework that holds these issues (long spanish, boring questions, repetion, etc)."

## A turn has two halves, and needs something to bid with (*Claude*)

1. **Take-up:** what the character does with what the learner just offered.
2. **Bid:** what the character offers back, leaving the learner something to answer.
3. **Supply:** something new to bid with: the place, the day, the character's own life, what the learner has said before. Without it, even well-made bids repeat.

## Taking up

- **Good:** builds on what the learner actually said. Nunzio, after Josh's "Mia nonno lavora in una giardina in San Francisco": "Ma guarda! Allora suo nonno fa il mio stesso mestiere."
- **Bad:** a token reply and back to its own subject; or no take-up at all. Don Chucho's "Ah, el sol, yo también…", then straight back to the tin.

## Good bids (*Claude*, from Josh's examples)

1. **Answerable** with what the learner has at their level.
2. **Goes somewhere:** the answer opens the next turn. Nunzio: "Che tipo di giardino cura, lo sa?", after Josh's grandfather.
3. **Comes out of the moment** and makes sense in the place.
4. **Gives, then hands over:** shares something, then leaves an opening.
5. **About both of them:** sometimes the character's world, sometimes the learner's, as the learner has told it.

## By level (Josh, with *Claude*'s reading)

- **Intermediate: bids both ways.** "bids are morre relevant for intermediate where the conversation is open."
- **Beginner: engagement.** "in beginnner I cant think of what you could do more than engineer for engagement"; earlier, "the learner bid is continuing to play." At Beginner the bubbles are the learner's bids.
- **The steps in between** (*Claude*): the Beginner steps could hand the bidding over little by little: answers to say back, then questions to ask, then a sentence to finish, then their own words. Step 6 is where the learner makes offers of their own, which is Intermediate.
- **The intro is a rest, and culture comes first** (Josh): "learning culture is more important than language in this game. Its easier and drives interest. No one is going to head to mexico weillding beginner level spanish.. But knowing geigraphy, culture, food, tradition is useful." "learning a language is exhausting, the intro refined is a passive exposure before returning to Dora the Explorer." At Intermediate: "it probably isn't as relevant for intermediate, unless even thicker on culture and lighter on personality." So the intro isn't a bid. It's the listening part of a visit's rhythm: listen, then play.

## The faults, in bid terms (*Claude*)

| Fault | Which part failed |
|---|---|
| Long turns, long Spanish | Bid: no hand-over, or buried under too much |
| Boring questions, Dora, "¿Lo ve?" | Bid: dead end, stock, repeated |
| "Are you new here?" | Bid: presumptuous |
| The scontrino | Bid: impossible |
| Asks, then carries on | Bid: buried |
| Ignoring what was said | Take-up |
| Replaying the last visit; nothing new on visit seven | Supply |
| Endings | Neither side has a good bid left; the learner's goodbye is a bid to take up |

## How to use it (*Claude*)

- **To find the fix,** ask which part failed:
  - a missed take-up → the turn's shape;
  - a bad bid → what the character is given, or a rule that's causing it;
  - no supply → memory and the visit's point.
- **Before adding a rule,** ask whether it makes bids better or just adds a habit. Most past patches were rules about surface (length, wording, setups), and several turned into habits ("mire", "¿Lo ve?", two goodbyes).
- **When listening:** did the character take up what I said, and did its turn give me something worth answering?
- **Keep it thin.** Take-up, bid, supply, and the short lists above. If it grows into categories that code picks from each turn, it will breed habits the way the per-turn setups did.
