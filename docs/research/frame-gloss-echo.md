# Phrasing, glossing and echoing in a second language: what the research says about the frame

2026-11-11 · Claude, for Josh · Combines two sources: Fuori's earlier research notes (cited by file) and a web search run this session (cited by link).

**Question.** Beginner 2 cluster 1 speaks one line of dialogue with Spanish or Italian pieces slotted into English (the frame), against the older pattern of a sentence in the language followed by its English (the echo). What does research say about glossing, echoing and phrasing like this, and what does it say about the frame?

**Short answer.** The research supports the frame's direction and does not test the exact thing. No study found compares a piece slotted into an English sentence against a sentence followed by its English, for beginners listening by voice. The evidence is on nearby practices, mostly reading, subtitles and classroom self-report. Josh's structural judgment (2026-11-11: the frame is one line of dialogue; with the echo you listen to the English) fits what the research shows about attention, but the research can't prove it for voice.

**Goal the findings are judged against.** The learner follows the whole line, hears the language pieces as part of it, and remembers some of them.

## 1. Findings, by practice

| Practice | What the research says | Fit to Fuori |
|---|---|---|
| **Gloss** (the translation beside the word) | L1 glosses help vocabulary learning, more for beginners than for higher levels; the edge of L1 over L2 glosses shows on immediate tests, not delayed ones ([Kim, Lee & Lee](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168820981394)). One gloss mode beats several ([multimedia glosses, second-round meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000169182400218X)). Glossing during reading, listening and reading-while-listening gave similar incidental gains in one meta-analysis ([reading-focused meta-analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/405432915_The_effects_of_glosses_on_English_L2_incidental_vocabulary_learning_through_reading_a_meta-analysis)). | Supports a gloss next to a piece. The delayed-test result says a gloss won't make it stick alone. Almost all of it is reading. No listening-specific meta-analysis was found. |
| **Echo, or sandwiching** (the language, then the English, sometimes repeated) | Practitioner guidance only: traced to the 1972 Bilingual Method and recommended for listening, with no experiment ([Cambridge paper](https://www.cambridge.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/WP_L1_ELT_CPELE_WEB.pdf)). | No evidence for or against. A long-standing technique, not a tested one. |
| **Translation drawing attention away from the L2** | Learners with bilingual subtitles spent more time on the L1 line than the L2 line, yet comprehension was as good as with L1 subtitles ([eye-tracking, SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/examining-the-effectiveness-of-bilingual-subtitles-for-comprehension-an-eyetracking-study/D3E6D4CDE5C5023CE085E95CA4D18ACE)). Evidence of extra cognitive load is mixed. | The closest support for "with the echo you listen to the English". It is about subtitles, not voice, and measures attention, not learning. |
| **Intra-sentential code-switching** (a piece slotted into a sentence) | Teachers switch for instructions, explanations and monitoring. Evidence is small studies and self-report, mostly positive for participation. Intermediate learners favoured it less than elementary learners ([CFL study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9015671/)). No controlled test of it against monolingual teacher talk was found. | The frame rests on this, and it is the least tested. |
| **Elaborated input** (rephrase, gloss, add) against simplified | Elaboration comprehends at least as well as simplifying ([Yano, Long & Ross 1994](https://core.ac.uk/download/32302657.pdf), reading). Elaborated input was best for comprehension and incidental vocabulary in the one listening study found ([Hillman, UMD](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/eee887c8-6db3-41d0-9dd3-f70c69280bf5/full)). Long 2020 ([optimal input](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/optimal-input-for-language-learning-genuine-simplified-elaborated-or-modified-elaborated/FDA2F787BFB75161F5D851C62BBFA7C9)) predicts elaborated and modified elaborated input beat overly hard unmodified input. | Supports the frame as elaboration. Doesn't say which elaboration. Same finding as [leveling/03](leveling/03-controlling-generation.md): "Research favours elaboration (repeat, rephrase, gloss) over stripping a line bare." |
| **L1 use in teaching** | Optimal-position view (L1 can improve L2 learning); [Zhao and Macaro 2016](https://cronfa.swan.ac.uk/Record/cronfa12349) (conference slides): a teacher's translation gave better retention than an L2-only explanation. One study found L2-only vocabulary teaching better. | Supports English doing a job beside the language. Mixed. |
| **Repeating aloud right after hearing** | Overt repeating gives a short-term gain resting on temporary representations. It beat shadowing for clips under about 2 seconds ([Mori 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688218771380)). Silent repeating didn't help. | Backs answers to say back more than the echo. |
| **Learner-first prompts: Pimsleur, Michel Thomas, Language Transfer** | A cue, a silent pause to produce the answer, then a confirmation. No peer-reviewed controlled trial found; claims are company-linked. | Design precedent, no validation. |

## 2. What Fuori's earlier research adds

From [beginner-mode-scaffolding.md](beginner-mode-scaffolding.md), [leveling/01-learning-science.md](leveling/01-learning-science.md), [leveling/03-controlling-generation.md](leveling/03-controlling-generation.md), [language-app-levels.md](language-app-levels.md) and [llm-language-learning.md](llm-language-learning.md):

- **Saying aloud beats silent reading** (production effect, also in L2). **Retrieving a phrase beats repeating it**: repetition matched retrieval for vocabulary and fell behind on grammar.
- **Chunks are the right unit for beginners.** Beginners lean on memorized phrases, and chunks are later unpacked into grammar.
- **Frames help and support should fade**; support that never changes creates over-reliance (small studies, mostly without controls).
- **Help on request beats simplifying ahead of time** (Loschky 1994): simplified input without interaction did no better than unmodified input; unmodified input plus negotiation did best.
- **Under a fifth of new words stick from listening alone.** Leveling makes the game playable; recycling, retrieval and help on request do the teaching.
- **Spacing helps only after enough repetitions**: echo first, then quiz.
- Shipping products give beginners tap-to-translate, suggested replies to read and hear before repeating, and replay at a slower rate; they shrink the learner's reply space, not the world.

Repo evidence that bears on the same question (our own tests, not the research literature): [beginner-deep-dive.md](../beginner-deep-dive.md) notes the English-then-language echo reverses the 5 Nov "say each thing once" fix, and that the English echo was the only dial that moved the English balance when measured.

## 3. What this means for the frame

1. **The structural point is the best-supported one.** Attention goes to the L1 line when both are present (subtitle eye-tracking), so a sentence followed by its English trains the learner to wait for the English. Not proven for voice; nothing contradicts it.
2. **Slot chunks of 4 to 8 words, not single words.** Chunks are the beginner's unit; the Level 1 emulations (2026-11-11) slotted mostly nouns, the weakest form.
3. **A gloss is worth having and won't be enough alone.** Retrieval is what makes pieces stick, so answers to say back matter more than extra volume. This supports restoring answers for cluster 1 (the live path has none since fe38bc2).
4. **No source gives the right share of language.** "A third" in the emulations was Claude's number, not the literature's.
5. **Measure slotted clauses of real length, not language words a turn** (Josh's refinement, [memory note](../../.claude/projects/-Users-joshpetersen-fuori/memory/beginner-structure-not-spanish-volume.md)).

## 4. Gaps and caveats

- No listening-specific meta-analysis of glosses; no test of slotting against echoing; no controlled study of intra-sentential switching for beginners.
- Mostly reading, subtitle and EFL classroom evidence; code-switching findings are largely self-report.
- Several sources are slides, dissertations or secondary summaries (Zhao and Macaro via a conference deck, the Cambridge sandwiching paper, Hillman via an abstract); full papers not read. Search metadata on dates was inconsistent in places.
- The repo notes were written earlier from their own searches; their findings are quoted as written, not rechecked.
