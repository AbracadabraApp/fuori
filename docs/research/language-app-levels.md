# Tell the character, then measure it

Language apps handle learner level in two very different ways, and the gap between them is the main lesson for Fuori. On the product side, the big apps keep placement short and optional, label levels with CEFR or a friendlier proxy, and lean on streaks rather than levels for retention. **None of them publishes how accurate its placement is**, and their CEFR claims rest mostly on company-authored studies of reading and listening, not speaking. On the technical side, Duolingo's models (Birdbrain, half-life regression, the Duolingo English Test) are real and well documented, but they need millions to billions of answers to calibrate. They don't transfer to a small app. What does transfer is the newer AI-conversation work, and it gives a clear answer to Fuori's B2+ problem. **Putting the level in the prompt works. Today Fuori's prompt gives characters no level at all; they only see the learner's transcribed words, and are left to infer the rest.** Even prompted level fades over a conversation unless it's re-anchored. And the cheapest reliable check of whether a line is too hard is the share of its words outside a graded word list, not a readability formula. For Fuori, the top moves are to add the learner's tier to every character prompt on every turn, as a concrete description worded in role; to build a CEFR judge calibrated against official exam material, which scales to any language with CEFR exams; and to make beginners comfortable by shrinking what they have to *say* (Josh's reply ladder) rather than only shrinking what characters say. Italian-specific evidence is thin throughout. Most findings come from English, Spanish and Japanese.

## Placement is a few minutes long and nobody validates it

The mainstream pattern is "start from zero" as the default, plus a short adaptive test for people who say they know some. Babbel offers its placement test automatically on first use. It takes "only a few minutes," can be retaken any time, and sends zero-knowledge learners to a "Language Level 0" Newcomer Course ([Babbel for Business](https://www.babbelforbusiness.com/blog/app-individual-levels/)). Duolingo's placement test is adaptive: "after every question, your skill level is estimated, and the next question is chosen based on this new estimate." But when it added partial credit in 2018, the author admitted the weights were **"pulled out of thin air"** rather than fit from data ([Duolingo Blog, 2018](https://blog.duolingo.com/partial-credit-improvements-to-duolingos-placement-test/)). Mid-course, Duolingo's "Jump Here" lets a learner skip ahead by passing a no-hints unit test. Failing sends them back to the start of that unit ([Lingoly](https://lingoly.io/duolingo-jump-here/)).

Rosetta Stone is the outlier. Its browser test runs **30–45 minutes** and returns a score, a CEFR level and an ability description. For Italian it works only in a desktop browser, and languages without a test go straight into Foundations, which covers A1–A2 ([Rosetta Stone Support](https://support.rosettastone.com/?p=978)). That length makes sense for its enterprise and school buyers, who need a reportable level. Consumer apps protect onboarding conversion instead.

The AI tutors replace testing with self-report. Praktika's onboarding runs past 30 screens of questions about level, motivation, speaking fears and practice time, then gives "a short vocabulary check" ([Copycat Cafe](https://copycatcafe.com/blog/praktika-review)). Those screens look built for conversion and personalization as much as accuracy. Memrise and Dreaming Spanish skip placement almost entirely. Memrise placed existing users into its new level system from their history ([Memrise Blog](https://www.memrise.com/blog/new-progress-tracking-for-language-learning)), and everyone on Dreaming Spanish starts at Level 1 with zero hours ([Palteca](https://palteca.com/dreaming-spanish-review)). The research found no public figures from any app on how many users take placement or how often placed learners turn out to be misplaced. Pimsleur, LingQ and Busuu's current flows couldn't be verified.

## Apps split ability from effort, and effort is what keeps people coming back

Level labels fall into four families: CEFR (Babbel, Busuu, Rosetta Stone), a proprietary number mapped to CEFR (the Duolingo Score), an effort metric (Dreaming Spanish hours, Memrise points), and plain beginner/intermediate/advanced names (Speak). The effort metrics only go up. Memrise's 31 levels run from "Absolute Beginner" to "Mastery" on points (a MemBot conversation earns 30, Level 31 needs 100,000). Memrise built it to replace weekly goals it called "confusing and restrictive," with the reassurance that "none of your previous efforts will be lost" ([Memrise Blog](https://www.memrise.com/blog/new-progress-tracking-for-language-learning)). Dreaming Spanish ties hours to abilities: Level 4 at 300 hours means understanding a patient speaker, and Level 5 at 600 hours means following normal native speech ([Palteca](https://palteca.com/dreaming-spanish-review)). Even its own reviewer notes the system "has no way of knowing how much vocabulary you've actually acquired."

Duolingo's move is the most revealing. The Duolingo Score runs **0–160**, with bands such as 30–59 for A2 and 100–129 for B2. It rises as lessons are completed, so **it measures course progress, not tested ability**, and its calculation and validation are unpublished ([Duolingo Blog, Oct 2024](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-score/)). Yet LinkedIn lets users display it as a credential ([heise.de](https://heise.de/-10651475)). The score borrows CEFR's credibility and the scale of the Duolingo English Test while giving learners frequent "+1" moments. That's a gap between progress and ability that Fuori should not copy.

Retention, meanwhile, comes from habits, not levels. Duolingo has run **more than 600 streak experiments**. Its retention PM says retention "is most fragile in the first seven days." Letting users pick a streak goal "significantly increased retention," and tying streaks to XP "confused users" ([Lenny's Podcast](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)). His blunt line, "streaks are an engaging tool, but they can't make up for a weak product," applies to level design too. Duolingo's 2022 switch from a branching tree to one linear path drew a public backlash ([UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/down-the-wrong-path-the-disaster-of-the-latest-duolingo-ui-update-a4cdd1e6ea1c)), and DAU kept growing anyway, passing 50 million with 36% year-on-year growth by Q3 2025 ([Duolingo 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1562088/000162828025049514/q3fy25duolingo9-30x25press.htm)). That's correlation only. No app has published A/B results linking placement, level labels or progression design to retention.

CEFR over-claiming is the trust risk underneath all of this. Duolingo's headline efficacy study, which found that finishing 5 units matched 4 university semesters, was authored by Duolingo employees and tested reading and listening only ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2022/01/12/2365800/0/en/Leading-Language-Research-Journal-Publishes-Study-Showing-Duolingo-Learning-Outcomes-Are-Comparable-to-University-Classes.html); [Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/effective-intermediate-learning)). Busuu's "22 hours equals a semester" study wasn't peer reviewed ([Wikipedia: Busuu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busuu)). For speaking apps, a reviewer warns that lenient speech recognition "can create a false sense of mastery" ([LanguaTalk](https://languatalk.com/blog/speak-app-review/)). The research turned up no independent study validating any app's B1/B2 claim.

Both ends of the scale are weak spots. Before its B2 expansion, most of Duolingo's nine big courses stopped at A2 ([Feedbagel](https://feedbagel.com/post/duolingo-expands-nine-language-courses-to-b2-proficiency-level)). Reviewers find AI tutors weakest for true beginners, who can't yet produce speech, and for advanced learners, who need precise feedback. One calls Speak "noticeably less effective from B1 level onwards" ([LanguaTalk](https://languatalk.com/blog/learn-english-with-ai-english-tutor/)). That reviewer runs a competing product.

## Duolingo's machinery runs on a billion answers a day

The technical stack behind Duolingo's levels is impressive and mostly irrelevant at Fuori's scale. Birdbrain predicts whether a learner will get a given exercise right, and the session generator uses that to pick exercises. Version 1 was a logistic model in the style of **item response theory (IRT)**: probability correct is a function of learner ability minus exercise difficulty, updated after each answer with an Elo-like step. Version 2 is an LSTM that compresses each learner's history into a 40-dimensional ability vector. Both were trained on about **1 billion exercises a day** from roughly 50 million learners, and neither has published accuracy figures ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo); [Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/learning-how-to-help-you-learn-introducing-birdbrain)).

Half-life regression (HLR) handles memory. It models recall as p = 2^(−Δ/h), where Δ is time since last practice and h is a learned half-life, and it was fit on 12.9 million practice traces. It cut prediction error about 45% against the Leitner box method. But the live A/B results carry a warning. **The simpler model without word-level features gave +12% daily retention over the full model**, and full HLR lost 7.3% on practice-session retention against Leitner ([Settles & Meeder, ACL 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf)). Offline accuracy didn't predict real-world benefit.

The Duolingo English Test is where the rigor lives. It calibrates items with a 2PL IRT model, runs a computer-adaptive test that updates an ability estimate after every item, reaches **test–retest reliability of r = 0.93**, and correlates with TOEFL at r = .71. Its machine scoring of speaking and writing matches human raters about as well as humans match each other (r = 0.84 vs 0.85 for speaking). It uses features such as the share of words at C1 and above, sentence-tree depth, words per second and speech-recognition confidence ([DET Technical Manual](https://www.peb.ufrj.br/images/documentos/DET-technical_manual-V01.pdf)). Its CEFR alignment came from an external 12-expert panel ([DET Blog](https://blog.englishtest.duolingo.com/duolingo-test-aligned-with-cefr)). That's what a defensible CEFR claim costs.

The general methods sort cleanly by how much data they need. Elo or Rasch updates with difficulties set by hand or by an LLM work from the first user, and Elo was proposed specifically for the cold-start problem ([ICCE 2020](https://library.apsce.net/index.php/ICCE/article/view/3939)). Bayesian knowledge tracing (BKT) needs skill-tagged items and hand-set parameters. Calibrated 2PL IRT typically needs hundreds of responses per item. Deep knowledge tracing and Birdbrain V2 need Duolingo-scale logs. Fuori has a deeper mismatch too: conversation has few right-or-wrong "items." Any IRT-style tracking would first need graded events, such as a judge's rating of each learner turn.

## Word coverage predicts confusion, and LLM judges can grade speech

Two measurement problems matter for Fuori: how hard a character's line is, and how good the learner's speech is. Both have cheap answers that need no user data.

For line difficulty, the strongest evidence favors word coverage over readability formulas. In a Japanese beginner study, the share of words not on the learner's graded list (the "token miss rate") tracked moments of not understanding at **ρ = 0.78. A standard readability formula managed ρ = −0.17** ([arXiv 2506.04072](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04072v2)). The classic coverage research points the same way. Listeners need about **95% of words known** for adequate comprehension, readers about 98%. Below 95%, comprehension varies widely between listeners rather than falling off a cliff ([Schmitt et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE/core-reader)). Those thresholds come from replayable story listening, so live speech probably needs more coverage, not less. In practice, a 10-word beginner line can carry about one new word.

For holistic level rating, a fine-tuned small BERT model rated short English conversational text better than a single human expert (MSE 0.37 vs 0.75). **A few-shot LLM came in at 0.48, while zero-shot was far worse at 1.45** ([Ace-CEFR, arXiv 2506.14046](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14046v1)). Examples in the prompt matter more than anything else. For Italian, the research found classification work from the Università per Stranieri di Perugia group (MALT-IT2, [LREC 2020](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2020.lrec-1.890)), but no ready-to-use sentence-level rater.

For the learner's speech, a 2025 method maps directly onto Fuori's pipeline. An LLM read Whisper transcripts and, for each of ten CEFR analytic aspects (fluency, grammatical accuracy, coherence and so on), picked the matching descriptor. **Zero-shot, it beat a fine-tuned BERT (r = 0.76 vs 0.73)**, though it trailed an audio model fine-tuned on spontaneous speech (0.82) ([arXiv 2507.10200](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10200v1)). The caveats are real. That data was English exam monologues, not short game turns. Transcripts carry no pronunciation information. And nobody has measured how well LLM CEFR judgments of short spoken turns agree with humans.

## Prompted level control works at first and drifts by turn nine

Duolingo's Video Call is the one AI conversation product with a detailed engineering account, and its design is instructive. The main prompt names the learner's CEFR level. A **separate LLM call writes the opening question**, because combining it with the main instructions would "overload the LLM," producing "overly complex sentences." During the call, checks ask whether the learner seems confused (if so, Lily rephrases) and whether the learner wants to lead the topic. After the call, an LLM extracts a list of facts for next time ([Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/ai-and-video-call/)). Course position sets call length, from about 1 minute early on to 3 later, and Lily doesn't correct grammar ([Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/video-call)). Every other product, including Speak, Praktika, Babbel Speak and Univerbal, says it "adapts to your level" without saying how. In practice, level comes from self-report or course position, not from measurement.

The research says prompting moves output in the right direction but doesn't hold. In a Spanish tutoring study, models prompted at A1, B1 and C1 started out correctly ordered, but **"the differences between system prompts consistently diminished over time"** across nine turns. The authors call prompting alone "too brittle for sustained, long-term interactional contexts" ([arXiv 2505.08351](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08351v2)). That study used 7–12B open models, not Claude. In the Japanese study with real beginners, learners failed to understand 60.6% of rounds with a simple prompt, **41.7% with a detailed prompt (level descriptions, sample dialogue, known expressions)**, the same 41.7% with "generate five and keep the easiest," and 16.7% with guided decoding ([arXiv 2506.04072](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04072v2)). Guided decoding needs token-level access that a closed API doesn't offer.

What goes in the prompt matters. For GPT-4 story generation, control error fell from 3.66 with no level, to 0.57 with the bare level, to 0.30–0.39 with descriptions or examples ([arXiv 2406.03030](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.03030v1)). For Arabic, **CEFR-matched syntax constraints gave the best match (0.91), the level name alone 0.74, and adding required vocabulary lists made it worse (0.65)**, because the model "builds sentences around required words." The model also overshot B1 toward B2 ([arXiv 2606.21981](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21981)).

User complaints confirm both directions of failure. Praktika users report "it is all in Spanish" on a first lesson, a tutor that "talks way too fast," and "every time I go into the app I have to tell AI that I a beginner." Meanwhile an experienced learner got "easy words" ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/praktika.ai?page=2)).

### Beginners are helped more by shrinking their replies than by simplifying the world

Shipping products make open conversation survivable for beginners with turn-level escape hatches: tap-to-translate, suggested replies to read and hear before repeating, slower or "turtle" replay, native-language fallback the AI still understands, and asking to repeat ([Langua support](https://support.languatalk.com/article/129-i-m-a-beginner-i-only-have-a-basic-level-in-my-target-language-is-langua-a-good-choice-for-me)). Duolingo Adventures uses multiple-choice replies, and its characters react to mistakes in character with confusion and "Oh, did you mean…?" ([Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/adventures)). The industry trend is toward *more* structure for beginners. Duolingo added coach-style Falstaff calls for beginners ([Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/beginner-video-call-with-falstaff)), and Babbel Speak deliberately avoids "complete conversational freedom" ([Babbel](https://www.babbel.com/press/en-us/releases/babbel-speak)). Pimsleur shows that cued production can start on day one: English gives the meaning, the learner answers in the pause, and a native model confirms ([Pimsleur analysis](https://courses.languagereactor.com/david/alc-english/raw/commit/adecabbc4d062182efd4cd12bf79b1138be11d98/pimsleur-study/ANALYSIS.md)).

The older interaction research favors elaboration over simplification. Beginners who could negotiate meaning understood best. Input simplified in advance didn't beat unmodified input, and repetition and rephrasing helped at least as much as simpler sentences ([Loschky 1994](https://www.k-state.edu/psych/vcl/documents/Loschky_2004_Comprehensible%20input%20and%20second%20language%20acquisition.pdf)). On correction, recasts made up 55% of teacher feedback in immersion classrooms but led to only about 18% repair. Prompts that push self-repair did better, but Lyster says they suit more proficient learners ([Lyster 1998](https://archive.carla.umn.edu/immersion/acie/vol1/May1998.pdf)). On speed, studies treat 110–135 words per minute as "slow" and 160–180 as average. Slower speech helped lower-level listeners and fast speech raised anxiety ([McBride](https://www.tirfonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/05/TIRF_DDG_2005_McBride_Complete1.pdf); [JALT 2001](https://jalt-publications.org/archive/proceedings/2001/532.pdf)).

## Implications for Fuori, ranked

The ranking weighs strength of evidence against how directly it addresses the B2+ problem and how well it fits the project rules.

### 1. Add the tier to every character prompt, every turn, worded in role (strong evidence)

Today the prompt contains no level information. The character gets the learner's transcribed words and nothing else, and the prompt assumes Claude will infer the level from them and adjust. It doesn't: the teacher's B2+ rating is what every study above would predict. This is not replacing a mechanism, since none exists; it's adding the missing input. Add two or three lines built per turn from the tier, worded as the situation a person in that role would notice, not as a teaching instruction. For example: *this person is new to Italy and their Italian is still basic; talk to them the way a patient [role] talks to a newcomer: short sentences, present tense, everyday words, and if a word is needed, say it and its English.* Draw the content from the sentence-shape rules already in `content/it/levels/`. Those files hold tense limits and sentence lengths (A1 at 3–5 words, A2 at 6–10), and the Arabic study found syntax constraints worked best. Leave out their word lists, which backfired there and also break the no-phrase-lists rule. Fuori already rebuilds the prompt each turn, which helps against drift. But earlier high-level lines in the conversation history still pull upward, so the level line should sit near the end of the prompt. This is a prompt change, so bump the version and add a changelog entry. For beginners, consider writing the opening line in its own small call, as Duolingo does.

### 2. Build the judge and calibrate it against official exam material (strong evidence for the method, unknown accuracy for Italian)

Without measurement, tier work is guesswork. Two cheap signals fit the language-neutral design. The first is a few-shot Claude judge that rates a character line on CEFR, with labeled examples per level kept as language data in `content/<country>/`. Calibrating against one teacher doesn't scale to more languages (Afuera already has no Spanish teacher), so take the examples and the answer key from official CEFR exam material: sample listening and reading texts from CELI and CILS (Perugia and Siena universities) and PLIDA for Italian, and DELE (Instituto Cervantes) for Spanish, graded A1–C2 by exam boards. The judge must rate those samples correctly before it is trusted; a new language needs only its own exam samples. One caveat: exam dialogues are scripted and tidier than casual speech, so the judge may rate natural talk slightly high. Zero-shot rating was three times worse than few-shot. The second is a word-coverage check: the share of a line's words outside the top N of a frequency list stored as language data. That was the best-validated predictor of beginner confusion. A frequency list measures the character's output, so it doesn't predict what learners say. Josh should still confirm he reads it as consistent with the no-phrase-lists rule. Then run it on today's characters: it should also say about B2+. The teacher becomes a spot check of sample conversations per tier, not the gate; useful where available, but nothing depends on one person.

### 3. Build the beginner ladder, and add negotiation in role (moderate evidence; the full ladder is unvalidated)

Josh's ladder (pick, read aloud, speak with help nearby, speak freely) matches what products converge on: shrink the response space, not the world. Each rung has precedent: Duolingo Adventures' choices, Langua's read-and-repeat suggestions, Pimsleur's cued production. No study validates the whole sequence or the rules for fading between rungs, so treat the transitions as experiments behind a setting. Fade on signals that work in any language: success on the previous rung, recording length and silence. Two additions are well supported. First, a confusion path that keeps the character in role. When the learner says they didn't understand, or goes silent, the character repeats the key word and rephrases, the way a barista would. Negotiation beat simplification in Loschky's study. Second, the "one slightly off" reply should get an in-character "did you mean…?" reaction, not a wrong-answer marker. Recasts suit beginners. Clarification requests ("Come?") can come in at intermediate, where self-repair pays off.

### 4. Let the tier drive speech speed and subtitles, and save them (moderate evidence, low cost; needs item 1's tier first)

Half of this exists. Speed already varies: English subtitles on gives 0.7, the default is 0.9, dialect on gives 1.0. But it follows the subtitle and dialect switches as a stand-in for level, so turning on English subtitles quietly slows the speech too, and none of the settings are saved: all three reset when the app reloads. What's left is to let the tier set speed and default subtitles directly, and save tier and overrides with the learner. The research supports slower speech for lower levels, and the plan's 0.7–1.0 relative range keeps the setting language-neutral. Map beginner to about 0.7–0.75, intermediate to about 0.85–0.9 and advanced to 1.0, keep the screen toggles as personal overrides, and store them with the learner. This is the cheapest change on the list and could ship first. The exact values are judgment calls, since no validated threshold exists for live Italian TTS.

### 5. Self-selected tier at start, Magda's judgment as the ability signal, progress kept separate (moderate evidence)

No app validates placement, and consumer apps keep it to minutes, so don't build a placement test. A tier question at the start, optionally followed by a short chat with Magda, is in line with the industry. Over time, the one stored level should come from evidence: Magda's judged level, plus a post-conversation judge that rates the learner's turns against CEFR descriptors aspect by aspect (the method that reached r = 0.76), plus timing signals already available (speech rate, silence). Combine them as a slow running estimate, so one bad day doesn't move it. Keep the plan's rule that Magda suggests moving up and the learner decides. Keep "how much of Italy you've seen" separate from "how able you are," which is the distinction the Duolingo Score blurs.

### 6. Consider a check-and-rewrite pass for beginner lines (weak evidence, real latency cost)

Guided decoding was the only method that stopped drift for beginners, and Claude's API can't do it. The nearest substitute is to check each beginner line with the coverage signal and rewrite it only when it fails. No paper tests a rewrite pass inside a dialogue loop, and "generate five, keep the easiest" did no better than a detailed prompt. Build this only if item 1 plus measurement shows beginners still getting lost, and run the check off the critical path where possible.

### 7. Show tier names, not CEFR claims, and reward effort rather than level (weak evidence for a small app)

Showing "A2" invites the over-claiming the big apps are criticized for, and Fuori has no validation to back it. Use the three tier names; "Advanced" is gentler than "Expert." Keep CEFR as the internal scale, and let only Magda talk about it, in teaching mode. For motivation, the evidence points to effort-based, always-rising signals and habit features rather than ability labels. Fuori's natural version is the world itself: days in the city, places visited, characters who remember you. Evidence here is weakest of all, since it comes from apps with millions of users and no published results on level design.

### Not worth building

IRT calibration, computer-adaptive placement, Birdbrain-style learner models, fitted half-life regression and deep knowledge tracing all need data Fuori won't have. HLR's own A/B results show that sophistication doesn't guarantee benefit. Revisit only if usage grows by orders of magnitude.

## Conclusion

The product research and the AI research point to the same conclusion from different directions. Successful apps fix the learner's level outside the conversation and hand it in, rather than expecting the conversation to discover it. Fuori's founding bet, that characters would infer the level from the learner's words alone, failed the way the studies predict, and the fix is cheap: give them the level. The harder problem is the beginner tier. Going from B2+ to A1 is too big a drop for prompting alone, and the 95% coverage threshold means a day-one learner can't follow Italian-only speech at all. For that learner, the `en` field, the reply ladder and the character's patience in role will carry meaning more than simpler Italian will. That may justify the plan's hunch that beginner needs two steps underneath one name.

Every claim in this report about whether a technique works for Italian, for short spoken turns or for Claude is untested, so Fuori needs its own measurement. A judge calibrated against official exam material scales to every language Fuori adds; the teacher's ratings and Josh playing Afuera as a true beginner are spot checks on top. Together they would give Fuori better evidence about its own levels than any app here has published about theirs.
