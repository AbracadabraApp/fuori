# Communicating in Mexico (for Afuera's characters)

2026-10-19 · **Source:** Hernandez-Pozas, Kelm and Victor, *The Seven Keys to Communicating in Mexico: An Intercultural Approach* (Georgetown University Press, 2020); Josh's copy, read in full. Summary in our own words; the Spanish examples are ours unless marked as a book saying.

**What the book is:** a guide for US and Canadian professionals working with Mexicans, built on the LESCANT model (Language, Environment, Social organization, Contexting, Authority, Nonverbal, Time). Most of it is about offices, negotiations and managers. The parts that matter for Afuera are chapter 1 (Mexican Spanish), chapter 4 (indirectness, sayings, diminutives) and the everyday bits of chapters 3, 6 and 7. Chapter 2 (geography, water, crime, altitude) and most of chapter 5 (the patrón system, corporatism) are background only.

**What it doesn't cover:** *ahorita*, everyday greetings like *buenas* or *provecho*, and shop talk like *¿qué le damos?*. Those items in "Everyday speech" are marked *(not in the book)*. They come from general knowledge, so check them with a Mexican speaker before relying on them.

## The seven keys

### 1. Language
- Mexican Spanish sounds polite and formal next to other varieties. People keep *usted* longer than a learner expects, and they think of it as respect and distance, not stiffness.
- Very polite wording is normal: *¿mande?* for "sorry?", and the book's example of a student answering "I couldn't tell you" (*no podría decirle*) instead of "I don't know."
- Mexicans forgive a foreigner's imperfect Spanish and like the effort. (Heritage speakers are the exception: they're often judged harshly.)
- Spanish shows emphasis with word order or *sí* (*ella sí sabe*), not with pitch. A learner who carries English pitch into Spanish can sound angry or overexcited.
- Pronunciation: strong consonants, a clear *s*, and many Náhuatl words (place names, food). English loanwords are common too, like *chamba* (job) and *lonche*.
- With limited Spanish, a learner sounds blunter than intended because the softening tools aren't there yet.

### 2. Environment
- Background for scenes, not for speech: big cities, two Mexicos (modern and poor), gated buildings and security everywhere, the card machine brought to your table, ATMs inside booths.
- In small towns, taxis have no meter, so you agree on the fare first. Drivers stopping to ask the way is normal, not a scam.
- People drink bottled water, not tap water. Mexico City is cool at night and in winter.

### 3. Social organization
- Family means the extended family, plus *compadres* (godparents), who are almost family.
- Society is collectivist and values harmony: no arguing in public, no making someone look bad. *Discutir* sounds like a fight, so people say *platicar*, *hablar*, *conversar*.
- The calendar matters: Mother's Day (May 10), Children's Day (April 30), Independence (Sept 15–16), Day of the Dead (Nov 1–2), Guadalupe (Dec 12), then posadas and the "Guadalupe–Reyes" run of holidays.
- Fútbol is a passion. Pick a side in a *clásico* (Chivas–América, Tigres–Rayados).
- Catholic faith runs through daily life (*si Dios quiere*, *gracias a Dios*), and so do regional pride and class differences.

### 4. Contexting (the key that matters most for Afuera)
- Mexico is high-context: the listener is expected to read hints, the setting and the relationship. Saying the obvious out loud can sound rude.
- People avoid open confrontation and bad news. They praise in public and correct in private, and protecting someone's *dignidad* matters a lot.
- They rarely refuse outright. "It would be hard to…", "we'll talk later", "I'll try to come" usually mean no, and everyone knows it. The book stresses this isn't lying, just keeping doors open and not imposing.
- Modesty: don't boast (*echarle mucha crema a los tacos* is a saying for exaggerating). Plans come with *si Dios quiere*.
- Silence is uncomfortable (the saying *¿te comieron la lengua los ratones?*), so someone fills it.
- Humor softens hard things, but only Mexicans get to joke about Mexico. Jokes about mothers or the Virgin are off-limits.
- Diminutives are a high-context tool: they show affection, soften requests, cushion bad news, show sympathy, and help in haggling.

### 5. Authority
- People show outward respect up the ladder: titles (*licenciado*, *don/doña*), *usted*, not interrupting or correcting a superior.
- Authority is personal and warm. People trust those they know and distrust strangers in power. Relationships ("who you know") get things done.
- More deference goes to older people, people of higher status, and to class differences in general.
- Mostly business and politics (patrón system, corporatism). For a newcomer's daily life it shows up as titles, *usted*, and helpful people who know someone.

### 6. Nonverbal
- Common gestures: the back of the hand raised for thanks (with *muy amable*), wagging the index finger for "no", a twist of the wrist for *más o menos*, rubbing fingers to say *apúrate*, tapping the elbow for "stingy".
- People point with their chin or eyes, not a finger. Lowered eyes can show respect across class lines.
- Greetings: one air kiss on the right cheek in casual settings, a firm handshake in formal ones, an *abrazo* with back pats between men who know each other. People touch arms while talking and stand close.
- People show happiness, warmth and excitement openly, but hide anger and sadness. Men rarely cry in public.
- People dress more formally than in the US. The book also covers colour and symbols (flag colours, alebrijes, skulls).

### 7. Time
- Time is polychronic: relationships come before the clock. Several things happen at once, meetings run over, and the next person waits.
- Work events roughly start on time. Social events start when the context says and have no set end.
- "I've got it covered" can mean "we'll deal with it if it comes up."
- The past is alive in the present (history, places, family stories). Showing interest in it earns respect.
- Leaving or going quiet after a relationship starts feels like betrayal. People expect you to check in now and then.

## Everyday speech

Habits that show up in ordinary conversation, with short examples of our own.

| Habit | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***¿Mande?*** | The polite "sorry?" / "yes?" when you didn't hear or someone calls you | *—Disculpe… —¿Mande?* |
| ***Usted* by default** | Respect and distance with strangers, staff, older people; *tú* comes later or with the young | *¿Qué va a llevar?* (not *¿qué vas a llevar?*) to a new customer |
| **Diminutives to soften** | Makes a request or a command gentle | *¿Me pasa la salsita?* · *Hágase a un ladito, por favor.* · *Un momentito.* |
| **Diminutives for affection / sympathy** | Warmth; compassion | *Ay, pobrecito.* · *mi hijita* |
| **Diminutives to cushion bad news** | Takes the edge off unpleasant talk | *Hay unos malitos por ahí en la noche.* |
| **Diminutives in prices / haggling** | Makes a price sound small | *Son nada más unos pesitos.* · *¿Me lo deja en cien?* |
| **Polite indirect refusal** | Saying no without saying it | *Uy, eso está difícil…* · *A ver si se puede.* · *Ahorita no tengo, pero mañana a lo mejor.* |
| **"I'll try" = no** | Declining an invitation kindly | *No sé si pueda, pero hago la lucha.* |
| **"We'll talk later"** | Leaves the door open | *Luego lo platicamos.* |
| ***Si Dios quiere*** | Plans stay humble | *Nos vemos el viernes, si Dios quiere.* |
| **Ultra-polite "I don't know"** | Not knowing, without being curt | *Fíjese que no le sabría decir.* |
| **Thanks for a favor** | Often with the raised-hand gesture | *Muy amable.* |
| ***Platicar*, not *discutir*** | Talking without implying a fight | *Estábamos platicando.* |
| **Emphasis with *sí*** | Stress without raising the voice | *Este sí está bueno.* |
| **Small talk before the task** | The relationship first, even briefly | *¿Cómo ha estado? ¿Y su familia?* before the order or the question |
| ***Ahorita*** *(not in the book)* | "Right now", "in a bit", or "maybe never", depending on tone and context; *ahorita no* is a soft no | *Ahorita se lo traigo.* · *Ahorita no, gracias.* |
| **Greetings *(not in the book)*** | Greeting the room or the person on arrival and leaving; *provecho* to people eating | *Buenas.* · *Buenas tardes.* · *Provecho.* · *Que le vaya bien.* |
| **Shop talk *(not in the book)*** | How vendors offer and serve | *¿Qué le damos?* · *¿Algo más?* · *A la orden.* · *Pásele, güera.* |

## For Afuera's characters

These are written so each one could be a sentence in a character prompt or a check in the conversation judge (`content/es-mx/prompts/quality.md`). **Where it goes:** **P** = the character prompt (`content/es-mx/prompts/character.md`); **D** = Mexico's data (`CityPantry.customs` / `localTouches`, character `personality`, or `content/es-mx/language.ts`); **J** = can be counted by the judge.

The character prompt's rule is "describe the situation, not how to talk." Most of these work best as **D**: a shopkeeper's personality or the city's customs, which shape how the model talks without adding speaking rules. Only a few need to be in the prompt itself.

### Should avoid

| # | Avoid | Check |
|---|---|---|
| A | A blunt, bare refusal or correction (*No. Eso está mal.*). | J (a new count, if needed) |
| B | Going straight to the transaction with no greeting or acknowledgment, like a vending machine (*¿Qué quiere?* and nothing else). | J |
| C | Using *tú* with a new adult customer in a shop, unless the character is young and the place casual. | J |
| D | Laughing along when the learner jokes about Mexico, mothers or the Virgin. Only Mexicans get to mock Mexico; a real person would cool slightly or change the subject. Self-mocking Mexican humour from the character is fine. | P (only if seen in testing) |
| E | Pushy or angry tones, raised voices, open irritation at a slow learner. Mexicans hide impatience. | J (alive / note) |
| F | Spain's forms in a Mexican mouth (*vosotros*, *vale*, *coger*, *ordenador*). | J · D (`level-words-es.csv` `mx` rows) |

### What doesn't apply to a newcomer's daily life

- Negotiation style, contracts, term sheets, "hard to implement = no" in meetings, and lawyers' planning (chapter 8). The underlying habit, the soft no, does apply.
- The patrón system, corporatism, caciques, office doors, business dress, and Mexicans' English as a status symbol (chapters 1, 5, 6). One small exception: a character might offer to "put you in touch with my cousin" to help with an apartment or a bank. That's the everyday face of "who you know" (D, personality).
- Statistics on geography, the economy, water and crime (chapter 2). Use them, if at all, only for scene details.
- Punctuality at work (chapter 7). What carries over is the social version: a friend who is late, a plan that slides, *ahorita*.
