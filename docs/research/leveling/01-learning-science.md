# Leveling: what the learning science says

2026-11-02 · Research agent, topic: learning science · Scope: Beginner (A1–A2) → Intermediate (B1). Builds on [language-app-levels.md](../language-app-levels.md) and [llm-language-learning.md](../llm-language-learning.md) and does not repeat them (the 95% coverage rule, Loschky 1994, the 110–135 wpm "slow" range, prompt drift and Duolingo's design are there). Read [00-what-we-know.md](00-what-we-know.md) first.

**The short version.** At A1–B1 the research describes a level less by the text and more by **how the other person talks**: slowly, clearly, with repetition and rephrasing on request. Word coverage is still the best single predictor of whether a listener follows, but it explains only about half of listening success. Making a line easier ahead of time helps less than letting the learner ask and get help. And understanding a line is not the same as learning from it: under a fifth of new words stick from listening alone. So leveling makes the game playable. Recycling, retrieval and help on request do the teaching.

## 1. The findings that matter most

**1. 95% word coverage is the working target for listening; 90% works on average but is risky.** Learners who knew 95% and 90% of the words in spoken stories scored about the same on average (7.65 and 7.35 out of 10). But results at 90% were spread much wider, so 95% is the safer target ([Schmitt et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE), reporting van Zeeland & Schmitt). For harder test listening, Stæhr put the line at 98%, and vocabulary size and depth predicted **about half** of the variation in listening scores ([Stæhr 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/876C52338E1998800443F38B64DD98AE/S0272263109990039a.pdf/div-class-title-vocabulary-knowledge-and-advanced-listening-comprehension-in-english-as-a-foreign-language-div.pdf)). *Evidence: moderate to strong. Mostly English, with recorded monologues the listener cannot interrupt.*

**2. Unrestricted conversation is out of reach for A1–A2 words.** In ordinary speech, about **3,000 word families** plus names give 95% coverage, and 98% takes 6,000–7,000 ([Nation 2006, via Schmitt et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)). A1–A2 learners know far fewer (see section 2). So a character who talks "naturally but simply" will miss 95% for a beginner. The words have to be held to what the learner knows, plus help. *Evidence: strong for English corpora; Italian and Spanish untested, but the gap is too large to close by chance.*

**3. The CEFR defines A1–A2 listening by the partner's behavior.** A1: "Can follow language which is very slow and carefully articulated, with long pauses for them to assimilate meaning". A1 interaction "is totally dependent on repetition at a slower rate, rephrasing and repair". A2 understands "provided people articulate clearly and slowly" and "provided they can ask for repetition or reformulation from time to time". B1 follows "clear standard language… on familiar matters" and "will sometimes have to ask for repetition" ([CEFR Companion Volume 2020, ch. 3](https://rm.coe.int/chapter-3-communicative-language-activities-and-strategies/1680a084b4)). So the A2→B1 step mostly removes conditions on the speaker. Speech goes from slow to normal-but-clear, and repair goes from constant to occasional. *Evidence: expert consensus, not experiment, but it is the standard that exams and textbooks follow.*

**4. Help during the conversation beats simplifying ahead of time.** In a classic study, simplified input without interaction did no better for moment-to-moment comprehension than unmodified input. Unmodified input plus the chance to negotiate meaning did best. All three groups learned, and comprehension differences **did not predict** learning differences ([Loschky 1994](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/comprehensible-input-and-second-language-acquisition/90596BB3C4297189FC221117F1E8B25F)). Long's later review favors "modified elaborated" input, which keeps natural language and adds redundancy and clarity ([Long 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/optimal-input-for-language-learning-genuine-simplified-elaborated-or-modified-elaborated/FDA2F787BFB75161F5D851C62BBFA7C9)). In a meta-analysis of word learning from spoken input, **interactive tasks beat non-interactive ones** ([de Vos et al. 2018](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item_3008448)). *Evidence: moderate; consistent across decades, but the studies are small.*

**5. Understanding is not learning: under 20% of new words stick from listening.** A 2023 meta-analysis found about **15%** of target words learned from listening on immediate tests and **13%** on delayed tests. Input made for learners (graded readers, teacher talk) produced much larger gains than input made for native speakers (g = 1.56 vs 0.71 immediately; 1.34 vs 0.44 delayed). Spaced exposure across sessions beat one session (g = 1.71 vs 0.58 delayed) ([Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa 2023](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0261444822000507)). *Evidence: strong (meta-analysis). This supports leveling. It also shows that leveling alone teaches little without recycling.*

**6. Slower speech helps lower levels, but only so much, and maybe not for long-term listening skill.** Elementary learners scored better at 127 words per minute than at 188. Moderately fast speech (about 200 wpm) hurt, but slowing below normal did not always help ([Griffiths 1990, 1992, reviewed in Hayati 2010](https://file.scirp.org/Html/3014.html)). Inserted pauses gave "slightly higher" scores that were not significant (Blau 1990, same review). Learners who controlled the rate understood better (Zhao 1997, same review). In one 13-session study, a group that practiced at natural speed improved *more* than a group that practiced with slowed speech ([Hayati 2010](https://file.scirp.org/Html/3014.html)). That study is small and had no control group. In processing studies, L2 listeners anticipate words at about 3.5 syllables per second. At high speed, that ability nearly vanishes ([Kukona et al. 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/278BF9E0002D444ACD441CF0B8512F1F/core-reader), citing Fernandez et al. 2020). *Evidence: moderate that slow helps comprehension now; weak and mixed on what builds listening skill.*

**7. Support that helps beginners later gets in the way (the expertise reversal effect).** Across about 27 comparisons with more than 2,200 learners, methods that helped novices lost their value or backfired as knowledge grew. Differences ranged from 0.45 to 2.99 effect-size units. Tutorials that adapted to tracked performance beat fixed ones (effect sizes 0.46–0.69). Kalyuga's prescription is to track knowledge as it changes and fade support step by step ([Kalyuga 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)). The scaffolding literature agrees. Good scaffolding is **contingent** (it responds to what the learner just did), it fades, and responsibility moves to the learner ([van de Pol et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-010-9127-6)). *Evidence: strong in general education; thin for L2 conversation itself. Fuori's English narration is a support of exactly this kind.*

**8. The zone of proximal development can be measured as how much help a learner needs.** Krashen's "i+1" was never defined in a way that can be measured. Even sympathetic reviews treat it as a metaphor ([Lichtman & VanPatten 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12552)). The sociocultural line makes it concrete. Aljaafreh & Lantolf (1994) ranked tutor help from implicit to explicit, from "something is wrong here" to giving the answer. They read development from the *least* help a learner needed, and from that amount falling over time ([summary](https://my.vanderbilt.edu/l2studies/?p=121)). The approach has been built into software ([Ai 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/providing-graduated-corrective-feedback-in-an-intelligent-computerassisted-language-learning-environment/BADDC5BCB3E8C333A601440155F5FE1B)). *Evidence: qualitative and case-based, but it is the most usable working definition of "a little above".*

**9. Readiness to move up should rest on delayed, repeated, productive success.** Learners can recognize more words than they can produce, and the gap widens for less common words ([Webb 2008](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/receptive-and-productive-vocabulary-sizes-of-l2-learners/603A8FD24DBAA2874163DD36D8BAACA3)). Success right after practice overstates learning. In an L2 vocabulary study, massed learners won during training, but every spaced group won ten days later ([SSLA 2024 replication of Cepeda et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C833408A4C3BAD939CA39EA734423BB7)). Successive relearning means retrieving something correctly in several spaced sessions, not once ([Rawson & Dunlosky 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484)). Theory and simulations put the best practice difficulty at about **85% success** ([Wilson et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4)). That result comes from model learners on simple yes/no tasks, so treat it as a rule of thumb. *Evidence: strong for spacing and delayed testing; the 85% figure is weak for conversation.*

**10. Newer LLM work confirms that level control is hard, and that matching a level that changes is harder still.** A 2026 system that adjusted difficulty mid-dialogue matched the target difficulty only 27–65% of the time by prompting, and fine-tuning helped. Human and LLM judges agreed poorly on single turns (κ = 0.24) but ranked systems similarly (ρ = 0.70) ([Morioka et al., SIGDIAL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.sigdial-1.13.pdf)). Prompting met grammar-level constraints only 39–43% of the time ([Glandorf et al. 2025](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2502.07544)). *Evidence: moderate; simulated learners and smaller models, not Claude.*

## 3. What this means for Fuori, ranked

**1. Write each tier as how a person talks to a newcomer, not as a rule list.** Use the CEFR conditions themselves. A1: very slow, pauses, repeats without being asked. A2: slow and clear, repeats or rephrases when asked. B1: normal but clear speech on familiar matters, repeats now and then. This matches the "situation, not shape" result in 00 and keeps prompts short. *Cost: small. Fit: high: any language, no lists, short.*

**2. Make help on request the main lever, and count it.** Loschky and de Vos both say negotiation does more than pre-simplifying. Characters should treat "what?", silence, or English as a request. Follow the Aljaafreh & Lantolf order: repeat → slower → rephrase → offer two choices → give the word with its English → give a whole answer to say back. Log how far down that order each turn went. The falling depth of help over time is the best evidence of growth that the literature offers. *Cost: medium. Fit: high if the signals are silence, recording length and the character's or a judge's reading, not keyword lists.*

**3. Measure coverage per conversation against the learner's words, aiming for 95% with glossed words counted as known.** Use the syllabus lemma list for the measure only, never in the prompt. Report it alongside the existing "lines followed" score. Fuori's current target, 90% of words in a line, sits below the research threshold. For a 10-word line it allows one unknown word, which is fine only when that word is glossed or obvious from context. *Cost: medium (the list has known gaps, see 00). Fit: good: measuring, not prompting.*

**4. Set speech rate by tier in syllables per second, checked on real audio, per country.** Treat ~3.5 syllables/s as a starting guess for A1 (uncertain) and natural rate for B1. Let the learner slow a line down (learner control helped, per Zhao). Don't keep beginners at slow speed indefinitely: raise the rate as help requests fall. *Cost: small. Fit: high.*

**5. Fade the English and the say-back answers by observed need, not by visit count.** The expertise reversal effect says fixed schedules will both drop learners too early (seen in the reply-button trials) and over-support those who are ready. The staged plan already exists. Change its trigger to "the last N turns needed no English" rather than "N conversations played". *Cost: small. Fit: high.*

**6. Recycle words across days on purpose.** With ~15% uptake from listening, the same words have to come back at spaced gaps. Characters who remember the learner can reuse yesterday's words; the Diario and Learn can test them. This, more than precise leveling, is what turns play into learning. *Cost: medium. Fit: high; it uses memory and the syllabus that already exist.*

**7. Move up on delayed, spoken success.** A defensible rule: in two or more conversations on different days, the learner answers the tier's question type without help in most turns (about 80–90%), and the help depth stays low. Use a unit's frames as the checklist. The game suggests, the learner decides (Josh's decision). *Cost: medium. Fit: good; the success rate comes from the help count, not from judging grammar, so Whisper errors matter less.*

## 4. What questions the direction

- **The current headline metric may track the weaker lever.** "Share of character lines a beginner can follow on first hearing" measures pre-simplification. The research says comprehension after interaction matters more, and that first-hearing comprehension differences did not predict learning (Loschky 1994). A character whose first line is a bit hard but who repairs well may teach more than one held to 90% plain lines. Suggest adding "understood after help, and how much help" as a measure.
- **Leveling makes the game playable; it does not by itself teach.** Under 20% of new words stick from meaning-focused input (Webb et al. 2023). If the plan is "get the level right and learning follows," the evidence says no. Spaced recycling and retrieval have to carry the teaching.
- **Matching the level precisely may matter less than assumed.** The average difference between 90% and 95% coverage was small. Vocabulary explains only about half of listening success. Topic familiarity, visual context and the chance to ask cover much of the rest. A roughly right level plus very good repair is probably enough at A2–B1. A1 is the exception: below about 90% coverage, results scatter widely.
- **Slow speech has a cost.** One small study found practice at natural speed built listening better than slowed speech (Hayati 2010; weak). Keeping beginners on slow speech for weeks may delay the listening they need in Italy or Mexico.
- **The Beginner format's spoken English is a support that will need to fade.** It fits the research for true beginners, but the expertise reversal effect predicts it will start to hurt. The fade should be tied to the learner's need, which item 5 covers.
