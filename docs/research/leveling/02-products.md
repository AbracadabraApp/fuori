# Leveling: how products place and adapt learners

2026-11-02 · Research agent 2 of 4 · Scope: Beginner (A1–A2) → Intermediate (B1). Builds on [language-app-levels.md](../language-app-levels.md) and [00-what-we-know.md](00-what-we-know.md); it doesn't repeat them. Claims from a company's own marketing, or from a competitor's review, are marked.

**In one paragraph.** No AI conversation product publishes a method that holds an open LLM conversation at a beginner's level by prompting alone. The one company that has said how it does it, Duolingo, measures every character turn with a CEFR predictor, rates a whole call by its *hardest* turn, and fine-tuned its model on simplified rewrites of calls that ran too hard. That moved "sessions made easier" from 47% to about 70%. Everyone else fixes the level outside the conversation, from course position or a self-chosen level, and gives the learner levers to pull it down. Beginners get a different, guided product (a coach, suggested replies, English), not a simpler version of the open chat. Placement is thin everywhere. What moves a learner up is either course progress or a count: hours, known words, or units done. The pieces Fuori can copy cheaply are measurement of every line in real play, a "target success rate" controller fed by signals that work in any language, and a per-learner record of words already met.

## 2. Patterns that recur

**1. The level is decided outside the conversation and handed in.** Busuu places each conversation in the course timeline, after the words it needs. Babbel Speak orders scenarios by pedagogy. Duolingo picks Falstaff or Lily by course position. Speak doesn't open roleplay until upper-elementary. None of them claims the conversation discovers the learner's level. The products that say they adapt live (Praktika, Talkpal, Jumpspeak) draw the most complaints that they don't, in both directions.

**2. Beginners get a different product, not a simpler chat.** Duolingo's answer for beginners is a *coach*. Falstaff suggests phrases and their translations and gives feedback in the learner's own language. Lily, the open conversation, starts at early intermediate: Duolingo's own study used Spanish learners at Duolingo Score 45–55. By the end of 2026 Duolingo wants Falstaff in every unit, beside Lily. Speak gives beginners tutor lessons and drills before roleplay. Langua calls suggested replies the feature beginners and A2 learners use most. Fuori's Beginner format (spoken English, a few words, one question, answers to say back) is already the industry's shape. The open question is the handover, and none of these products publishes how it decides when a learner is ready for open chat.

**3. Prompting alone doesn't reach the low end; the leaders measure every line and then train.** Duolingo tags each turn with a CEFR predictor and rates the whole call by its hardest turn. It then collected calls that ran too hard, had the replies simplified, and fine-tuned on those. That made about 70% of sessions easier, against 47% for the base model (Natalie Glance, KDD 2025; paraphrased talk summary, no paper). Two 2026 papers say the same from the outside. In Alibaba's dialogue study, a CEFR-prompted model only spanned a difficulty range of 0.21–0.69, while the trained model spanned 0.07–0.88. Prompting squeezes the range, and the low end is what beginners need. In a SIGDIAL 2026 study, the best prompt-only method put the instructions and the dialogue history together in one user message with JSON output. Training with preference pairs still did better. Fuori can't fine-tune Claude, so the nearest copies are measurement, examples, and a check on the output.

**4. Vocabulary is the strongest lever, and length rules hurt.** In the Alibaba study, the share of simple words was the feature whose removal hurt control most. Removing the sentence-length constraint *improved* the overall score, because length limits made dialogue worse. Graded readers size books by cumulative headwords. LingQ, Lingvist and Language Reactor all work at the level of single words. Duolingo's word-level CEFR checker is only about 53% exact, though, and its Italian labels came from translation. Fuori's own trials found the same: frequency lists misjudge everyday words, and the syllabus word list correlated only 0.28–0.36 with the judge. The lesson isn't "use a list". The words that count are the ones *this learner* has met, not the ones a level is supposed to know.

**5. The learner can always pull the level down, and products that don't allow it get complaints.** Langua has a speed slider, a slow-replay button, suggested replies and "ask me to repeat." Univerbal added a change-level button even though it places by conversation. Praktika's reviews show what happens without such a lever: the learner has to argue the tutor down each session. Fuori has the Settings level and "say it in English." The research doesn't show that more toggles are better. It shows that a stuck learner needs one obvious way out.

**6. Adaptive systems aim at a target success rate, not at a level.** Math Garden picks items so children get about 75% right. Pelánek's Elo systems do the same with a "target success rate" setting. The DET's Interactive Speaking chooses each next question from the last answers. Elo needs one number per learner and one per item; with item difficulty known, about 10 answers give a usable learner estimate. Item difficulty needs about 100 learners to calibrate, unless it's set by hand. Fuori's 90% comprehension target is a target success rate, and the right controller is a simple one that steps down after misses and up after a run of successes.

**7. Progress is counted; ability is barely measured.** Dreaming Spanish counts hours. LingQ counts known words. Graded readers count headwords. Memrise counts points. Duolingo's Score rises with lessons done. Only the testing products (DET, Rosetta Stone's long test) measure ability, at a cost no consumer product pays. Counting is what Fuori's staged plan already does ("count practice, not visits").

**8. Almost nobody publishes evidence.** Duolingo's March 2026 Video Call report says beginners using Falstaff spoke useful phrases better and intermediate users improved more on a speaking test. It gives no effect sizes or sample sizes. No other AI conversation product found here publishes level-matching numbers. Most review sites are run by competitors (LanguaTalk sells Langua; Palteca competes with Dreaming Spanish).

## 3. Implications for Fuori, ranked

**1. Score every character line in real play, and rate each conversation by its hardest line.** *Cost: small. Fit: good (the judge is language-neutral; nothing predicts learner speech; the prompt doesn't change).* This copies Duolingo's KDD method with the judge Fuori already has (`level.md`, `lib/level.ts`). 00-what-we-know notes there is no level score on real play and no measure of the live Beginner format against the 90% target. Run it off the critical path after each reply. Report two numbers per conversation: the share of lines a beginner can follow, and the worst line. That turns "10–25%" into a live figure for today's Beginner format. Measure only the target-language parts of a Beginner reply; the English framing is support, not input to grade.

**2. Add a target-rate controller fed by "lost" signals that work in any language.** *Cost: small to medium. Fit: good, no phrase lists.* Count per conversation the turns where the learner seems lost: silence until the mic closes, a reply in English (Whisper's detected language is already read in `lib/speech/providers/whisper-groq.ts` for onboarding), a "say it in English" tap, or a judge-flagged "asked for help." Aim for at most about 1 lost turn in 10, the same 90% Fuori already chose. Too many lost turns steps the character's level down a notch *for the next conversation*; a run of clean conversations steps it up. This is the Elo/Math Garden idea with hand-set difficulty, so it needs no calibration data. It also gives the Beginner → Intermediate move a signal based on what the learner *understands*, beside the plan's count of practice. Products rarely adapt mid-conversation, and Fuori's drift results suggest between-conversation steps are the safer first version.

**3. Keep a per-learner record of words met, and use it to check lines, not to write them.** *Cost: medium. Fit: good, but confirm with Josh.* It measures the character's output against the learner's own history, so it doesn't predict learner speech. Fuori already logs phrases shown (`phraseStats`, about 105 a Beginner conversation) and has syllabus units and the Diario. A LingQ-style "met" set makes the coverage check personal: a line is easy for *this* learner if nearly all its words have been met or are A1 core. This replaces the level word list that failed and avoids the Arabic study's finding that required word lists in the prompt make output worse. One warning from graded readers: their Beginner band totals 300 headwords. If a Beginner conversation really shows around 100 new phrases, that is far more new language than any graded scheme introduces. Worth checking how many are actually new.

**4. Put two or three "too hard → simplified" pairs in the prompt as examples, stored as content data per country.** *Cost: medium. Fit: fair; it adds prompt length.* This is the closest a closed-API product gets to Duolingo's fine-tune: take real lines that item 1 flags as too hard, have them rewritten in role, and show the pairs. Few-shot beat zero-shot for CEFR rating in earlier research, and the SIGDIAL study found examples and dialogue in one message the steadiest prompt-only form. Earlier Fuori trials found that "describe the person" beat rules. Examples are a third option, not yet tried. Run it as a prompt trial and log it in `docs/prompt-trials.md`.

**5. Treat Intermediate as the "Lily" step, gated on syllabus position, with question kind as the main dial.** *Cost: medium. Fit: good.* Busuu, Babbel and Duolingo all hand the learner open conversation only after the course has given them the language for it. For Fuori that means the Beginner → Intermediate move happens at a unit count *and* the controller's comprehension rate. The first Intermediate block (none exists yet) should lean on question kind (choice, then one fact, then open), which the DET also uses as its adaptive lever. Avoid sentence-length rules, which hurt dialogue in the Alibaba study.

**6. One obvious way to make it easier, remembered.** *Cost: small. Fit: good.* The Settings level and "say it in English" exist. Make sure a lowered level sticks across sessions, and that the controller in item 2 never fights a level the learner chose. Don't add new toggles until someone asks (Josh's rule).

**Not worth building:** fine-tuning (not available for Claude in this setup), IRT or computer-adaptive placement (needs hundreds of responses per item), a long placement test (no consumer product validates one), or hours as the level (an effort count, not ability).

## 4. Findings that question the project's direction

- **The 90% target may be out of reach for open Italian by prompt alone.** Duolingo controls its own model, measures every turn, and still needed fine-tuning to get about 70% of sessions "easier." That's not even 70% at the target. Prompting squeezes the difficulty range at both ends. Every product that serves true beginners does it with a coach, suggested replies and the learner's own language. Fuori's Beginner format is that answer. So the 90% goal should be judged on the target-language parts of Beginner replies, and the open-chat 90% goal should start at Intermediate. Measuring the live Beginner format (item 1) comes before any more prompt work.
- **Simulated learners probably flatter the results.** An ACL 2026 study found that prompted LLM "students" are too competent, ask too many questions, and vary too little; fine-tuning only partly fixed it. Fuori's tuning loop (test:conversations, the synthetic month, placement.mts) rests on simulated learners, and the placement test already failed 18 of 18 when the simulated learner didn't answer as itself. Real-play signals (items 1 and 2) should become the main evidence; simulated runs should be used to compare prompt versions, not to claim a percentage.
- **Live adaptation inside a conversation is rare and unproven.** Only Duolingo's confusion check and the DET's question choice adapt live, and neither publishes results. Moving the level between conversations is what the evidence supports.
- **The input-first camp says beginners shouldn't speak yet.** Dreaming Spanish's roadmap delays speaking to about 600 hours (as quoted by a competitor). Duolingo's Falstaff results and Pimsleur support early guided speaking, and neither side has strong public data. This doesn't overturn voice-first, but it supports keeping Beginner speech to answers the learner has just heard.
