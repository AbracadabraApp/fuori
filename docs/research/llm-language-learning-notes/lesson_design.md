# Evidence-based design of short one-to-one spoken language lessons (~10 min) and linking practice to real-world use

Scope note: about 17 search/fetch calls. Several primary papers were paywalled (Kim & Webb 2022, Spada & Tomita 2010, de Jong & Perfetti 2011, Webb 2007), so some figures come from abstracts or secondary summaries. These are labelled. Claims from background knowledge that I could not verify this session are kept in Gaps, not stated as findings.

## 1. How much can be learned or consolidated in one short session?

### Takeaway
No source sets a number of new items for a 10-minute spoken lesson. What the evidence does support: one session gives exposure, not mastery. Words need many spaced encounters, and gains come from spreading practice across sessions rather than packing more into one. A one-goal lesson (one structure or function plus a handful of words) that gets revisited later fits the evidence better than a big lesson.

### Cited Findings
- Learners commonly need to meet a new word about 6–20 times before it is remembered (from extensive-reading research: Rott 1999; Zahar, Cobb & Spada 2001). This is a teacher-resource summary, not a meta-analysis — [HK EDB vocabulary resource](https://cd1.edb.hkedcity.net/cd/eng/vocab09/ch1_3-3.htm)
- Webb (2007, *Applied Linguistics*, "The effects of repetition on vocabulary knowledge") is the standard source on repetitions. How much is learned depends on both the number of encounters and what the learner does with the word, and few studies test both together — [Hispadoc study citing Webb 2007](https://hispadoc.es/descarga/articulo/5412410.pdf); [USAL record](https://produccioncientifica.usal.es/documentos/5e4fc3e329995245c6b2e7dd)
- Spacing beats massing for verbal recall (Cepeda et al. 2006: 839 assessments, 317 experiments, 184 articles). The best gap between sessions grows as the retention interval grows. So for long-term retention, spreading the same practice across days beats doing more in one sitting — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/); [PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)

### Inferences
- A 10-minute spoken lesson should introduce at most one target structure or function plus a small set of words (my estimate: about 3–6, not measured), and should plan for those items to come back in later lessons and scenes. With 6–20 encounters needed, a single lesson cannot reach "learned"; a realistic in-lesson goal is several successful productions, followed by spaced recall next time.
- The "recall from last time" opening is the main way a lesson consolidates earlier material. It is probably worth more than adding new items.

### Gaps
- I found no study on how many new L2 items or structures adults can take in during a 10-minute spoken session, and none on attention spans in spoken L2 practice. Working-memory and cognitive-load limits (e.g., Cowan's ~4 chunks; Sweller's cognitive load theory) are relevant background but were not verified this session.
- The full text of Webb (2007) was not available, so the encounter thresholds it reports for different kinds of word knowledge are not given here.

## 2. Retrieval practice, spacing and interleaving for L2

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (getting the learner to produce from memory rather than repeat) is one of the most robust findings in learning science, with moderate general effect sizes. Spacing is confirmed specifically for L2 by a meta-analysis (Kim & Webb 2022, 37 studies), though the effect varies by skill and some subgroups are small. Interleaving evidence for L2 grammar is mixed and weakest for speaking.

### Cited Findings
- General testing-effect meta-analyses: Rowland (2014), Adesope et al. (2017), Schwieren et al. (2017). Practice testing beats restudying and other comparison conditions. Reported effect sizes are around Hedges' g = 0.50–0.63 — [Review of Educational Research, Adesope et al. 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306); summary in [Hogrefe article](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1024/1421-0185/a000229)
- L2-specific evidence: retrieval practice helps the learning of L2 grammatical patterns and vocabulary in both artificial and natural languages (Hopman & MacDonald 2018; Keppenne et al. 2021) — [CUNY dissertation](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_etds/5083)
- Artificial-language grammar study (single study): retrieval practice gave a long-term benefit and a far-transfer advantage at a one-week delay, which the authors attribute to learning the underlying rule rather than memorising items — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11461721/)
- Caveat for voice: when recall is spoken aloud, the benefit of retrieval is confounded with the benefit of saying the words aloud. Recognition and recall are not equivalent tests — [CUNY dissertation](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_etds/5083)
- Kim & Webb (2022), *Language Learning* 72(1):269–319, meta-analysis of 37 L2 experiments: confirms spacing helps in general, but the effect differs by L2 area, learner and practice activity, and some analyses are inconclusive because they rest on few studies. Effect sizes not retrieved (paywalled) — summary in [Serrano 2022, SSLLT](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602); [diposit.ub.edu](https://diposit.ub.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/911f8da8-9666-4917-9411-c9ae6754cd7b/content)
- Lag effects (short vs long gaps) in L2 grammar are mixed across studies, even though most L2 studies favour spaced over massed practice — [SSLLT 2024/25 article](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/49487)
- Interleaving for L2 grammar: Pan et al. (2019) found interleaved Spanish verb-conjugation practice better at a one-week delay. Suzuki & Sunada (2020, English relative clauses) found hybrid practice best immediately but no significant difference after a week. Commentary notes interleaving seems less effective for speaking practice — [Univ. Barcelona thesis](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/216425/1/245169.pdf); [Toulouse seminar notice](https://sciences-du-langage.univ-tlse2.fr/accueil/manifestations/seminaire-lnpl-interleaving-for-l2-grammar-learning-is-mixing-structures-beneficial-maud-pelissier-altae-urp-3967-universite-paris-cite)
- Suzuki and colleagues' "desirable difficulties" framework for L2 practice sets the level of challenge using three sources of difficulty: linguistic, learner-related and practice condition. Spacing and interleaving fall under practice condition — [same thesis](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/216425/1/245169.pdf)

### Inferences
- The opening recall should be retrieval (the teacher sets up a situation and the learner has to say the phrase), not a reminder followed by repetition.
- Bring last lesson's target back in the next lesson and again a few days later. Expanding gaps are reasonable, but the L2 evidence on exact gaps is mixed, so don't over-engineer the schedule.
- Within a 10-minute lesson, do the drill on the single target first, then mix it with earlier items in the role-play (a light hybrid). Avoid heavy interleaving of new structures in speech.

### Gaps
- Kim & Webb's moderator effect sizes (vocabulary vs grammar, gap between sessions, age) were not retrieved.
- Karpicke & Roediger (2008), on repeated retrieval in foreign-language vocabulary learning, and Bjork's "desirable difficulties" are classic support but were not fetched this session.

## 3. Mastery criteria and good stopping points in a drill

### Takeaway
No validated L2 rule says "stop after N correct productions." The best-known success-rate target, the "85% rule," comes from machine-learning models and is not an empirical human L2 result. Its useful message is that there is a sweet spot: practice that is neither too easy nor too hard. Practical stopping rules have to combine a success threshold with spacing: stop the drill once the learner produces the item correctly on their own a few times, and rely on later retrieval rather than over-drilling now.

### Cited Findings
- Wilson et al. (2019, *Nature Communications* 10:4646): for gradient-descent learners on binary classification, the best training error rate is about 15.87%, i.e. about 85% accuracy. It was shown in artificial neural networks and biologically plausible models — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- The lead author said the exact percentages, which came from simple computer tasks, matter less than the fact that a sweet spot exists. Applying the rule to human education is an extrapolation — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/learning-optimized-15171/); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/brain-waves/201911/the-right-level-of-struggle-for-learning)
- Spacing research implies diminishing returns from continuing to mass practice in one sitting compared with returning later — [Cepeda et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)
- Task-repetition meta-analysis on writing: more repetitions generally gave larger effects, and longer gaps between repetitions gave smaller ones. This is the only quantitative evidence I found on number of repetitions — [Abdi Tabari 2025 via todoele](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/effect-task-repetition-l2-learners-writing-performance-meta-analysis)

### Inferences
- A workable heuristic for the AI teacher (design inference, not tested). Move from drill to role-play when the learner has produced the target correctly without a model about 2–3 times in a row. End the lesson after a successful role-play use plus a real-world suggestion. If success stays well below about 70% after several attempts, simplify or step back, since the item is too hard right now. If success is near 100% from the start, the item is too easy, so add a twist (new context, a different person or number) or move on.
- Use "desirable difficulty" in a controlled way: wait a little before giving the learner the answer, and fade prompts. Prompts outperform recasts for durable gains (see section 7).
- End on a success, both for motivation and so that the last thing produced is correct. This is an inference; I found no source on it.

### Gaps
- No empirical L2 study validating a specific in-session success rate or criterion (e.g., "3 correct unprompted") was found.
- Successive-relearning work (Rawson & Dunlosky: reach a criterion within a session, then relearn across sessions) is highly relevant but was not fetched.

## 4. Task-based language teaching: pre-task, task, post-task; focus on form; effectiveness

### Takeaway
TBLT programmes show positive effects in meta-analysis. The original estimate (d = 0.93) has been revised down (g = 0.61), and critics argue many included programmes are really "task-supported." Explicit instruction outperforms implicit instruction in older L2 meta-analyses, which supports a structure where a brief explicit focus on form surrounds a meaningful task: teach and drill, then role-play, then feedback.

### Cited Findings
- Bryfonski & McKay (2019), 52 studies of TBLT programmes: overall d = 0.93 across learning outcomes, with moderators including region, institution type, needs analysis and cycles of implementation — [todoele record](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/tblt-implementation-and-evaluation-meta-analysis)
- A reanalysis found g = 0.61 and raised problems with inclusion criteria and effect-size calculation. Only sample size and whether the teacher was also the researcher explained heterogeneity — [LTR 2026 vol 30(3)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/13621688231167573); [todoele](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/meta-analysis-estimate-relative-effectiveness-tblt-programs-are-we-there-yet)
- Boers & Faez (2023) examined the 27 comparative studies and argued that all but one were unsuitable for the meta-analysis's purpose. Many would fit better as "task-supported" teaching — [todoele](https://todoele.net/node/442136)
- Norris & Ortega (2000, *Language Learning* 50:417–528): explicit instruction d = 1.13 (k = 71) vs implicit d = 0.54 (k = 29). These figures come from a secondary slide deck. Shin (2010) critiques the effect-size methods — [Columbia SALT, Shin 2010](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434); [slide deck](https://www.slideshare.net/uranoken/replication-27585404)

### Inferences
- Fuori's lesson shape (recall, teach and drill, role-play, real-world task) is closer to task-supported teaching (present, practise, produce with a task at the end) than to strong TBLT. The evidence supports this hybrid at least as well as pure TBLT, especially for adults, who benefit from explicit focus.
- Treat the role-play as the "task" with a clear goal (get the ticket, order the coffee). Give recasts and prompts during the task and a one-line post-task focus afterwards.

### Gaps
- Spada & Tomita (2010) and Goo et al. (2015), more recent explicit vs implicit meta-analyses, were not retrieved.
- Ellis's and Long's pre-task / task / post-task design guidance was not fetched; it is a framework description rather than effect-size evidence.

## 5. Linking practice to out-of-class, real-world tasks

### Takeaway
Being immersed abroad helps (meta-analytic g = 0.87), but there is little rigorous evidence on whether classroom-assigned real-world tasks transfer or get done. Small studies suggest communicative, goal-oriented out-of-class tasks get completed more than traditional homework. Transfer depends on whether learners see the outside task as similar to what they practised.

### Cited Findings
- Tseng et al. (2024, *LTR*), multi-level meta-analysis of study abroad (42 studies, 283 effect sizes, 1995–2019): g = 0.87 overall. Gains were largest for lower-proficiency learners who combined formal courses with homestays, and longer programmes gave larger gains — [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/revisiting-the-effectiveness-of-study-abroad-language-programs-a--2/)
- Single study (adult EFL, Costa Rica 2013): replacing traditional homework with communicative out-of-class tasks raised completion rates and encouraged learners to reflect on their learning — [Barrantes Torres et al. 2013](https://www.revistas.ucr.ac.cr/index.php/rlm/article/view/12609)
- Single study (Chinese study abroad, 2020): learners who consistently completed "field performance" tasks took part more in local life and were more confident speaking with native speakers — [JNCOLCTL 2020](https://doaj.org/article/155531885d07456d9df128f0e2ee6dfd)
- Single study (James 2008, ESL writing): whether skills transferred to an outside task depended on how similar learners thought the task was to their course — [CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994609487360)
- Survey (Romanian undergraduates, 2019): learners preferred self-directed out-of-class learning to workbook homework — [Pecican 2019](https://www.dialogos.rei.ase.ro/36/15.Pecican.pdf)

### Inferences
- Make the real-world suggestion closely match the role-play: the same phrase, the same kind of place, today or tomorrow. Perceived similarity seems to drive transfer.
- Keep it to one small, specific, low-stakes action. Follow it up in the next lesson's recall ("Did you try it at the bar? What did they say?"). That makes the real-world attempt part of the retrieval loop and gives accountability, which is likely to increase how often learners actually do it (inference).
- Fuori's persona (living in Italy for a while) resembles a homestay study-abroad setting, the setting where the meta-analysis found the biggest gains, especially at lower proficiency.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis was found on how often language learners complete assigned real-world tasks, or on whether a lesson-to-street link improves outcomes. Implementation-intention research (Gollwitzer: if-then plans raise follow-through) is plausibly relevant but was not verified.

## 6. Fluency (4/3/2, repetition) vs accuracy in short sessions

### Takeaway
Repeating the same task under rising time pressure (4/3/2) reliably improves fluency, and the gains can last. Several studies find no matching accuracy gain. Task-repetition meta-analyses (2025) show repetition helps across complexity, accuracy, lexis and fluency, with accuracy gains strongest when feedback is added. Which measure benefits most varies by meta-analysis and by speaking vs writing.

### Cited Findings
- Nation (1989) popularised 4/3/2 (originally Maurice 1983) and found gains in speaking rate. De Jong & Perfetti (2011): high-intermediate ESL learners who repeated the same talk in 4, 3 and 2 minutes gained more fluency than learners who gave talks on three different topics, and the gains held in the short and long term (from secondary sources) — [Kwansei repository](https://kwansei.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/30914/files/2.pdf); [LearnLab wiki](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fluency_Summer_Intern_Project_2010)
- Boers found 4/3/2 gains limited to fluency, not accuracy; another study saw no change in accuracy or content. Earlier studies used advanced ESL participants — [UCL / LTR 2021](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10128571/1/LTR2021.pdf); [Kwansei](https://kwansei.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/30914/files/2.pdf)
- Oral task-repetition meta-analysis (Abdi Tabari et al., *System* 2025): large effects on syntactic complexity and accuracy, smaller on fluency and lexical complexity, with larger effects in foreign-language than second-language contexts — [Hildesheim repository](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19111); [LTR TR meta-analysis PDF](https://todoele.net/sites/default/files/2025-12/LTR-effect-of-task-repetition-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Writing task-repetition meta-analysis (Abdi Tabari 2025, 31 studies): accuracy d = 1.19, the largest effect. More repetitions helped; longer gaps between repetitions reduced effects; adding feedback improved accuracy but reduced lexical complexity — [todoele](https://todoele.net/bibliografia/effect-task-repetition-l2-learners-writing-performance-meta-analysis)
- Liu & Tang (2025, *LTR*, 17 writing studies): overall g = 0.25, fluency g = 0.51 — [todoele](https://todoele.net/node/427836)

### Inferences
- In a 10-minute lesson, repeat the role-play once or twice with small variations (a new clerk, a new destination). This is cheap repetition that builds fluency, and pairing it with corrective feedback adds accuracy.
- Use time pressure lightly (the character "is busy") on a second run-through rather than in the first attempt.

### Gaps
- The meta-analyses above were read from abstracts and snippets. They disagree on which measure benefits most, and none looked specifically at 10-minute one-to-one spoken sessions.

## 7. One-to-one tutoring evidence and what effective tutors do

### Takeaway
Bloom's "2 sigma" is not supported at scale. Human tutoring is about 0.79 SD over no tutoring (VanLehn 2011), and K–12 tutoring programmes average about 0.29–0.37 SD (Nickow et al.). Step-based intelligent tutors come close to human tutors (0.76). In L2, oral corrective feedback has significant, durable effects, and prompts (pushing the learner to self-correct) beat recasts, especially for free production.

### Cited Findings
- VanLehn (2011, *Educational Psychologist*): human tutoring d = 0.79 vs no tutoring, far below Bloom's 2.0. Step-based ITS 0.76; substep-based 0.40 (figures from secondary sources and VanLehn's own slides; original paper not fetched) — [MIT symposium slides](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf); [arXiv survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.09628)
- Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan: pooled 0.37 SD (2020 working paper), revised to 0.288 SD (published 2024). Effects are larger for teacher and paraprofessional tutors, in earlier grades, and during the school day. Literacy subset 0.35 — [NBER w27476](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476); [EdWorkingPapers](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai20-267); [REL Appalachia](https://ies.ed.gov/rel-appalachia/2025/01/slide-deck-0)
- Lyster & Saito (2010, *SSLA* 32:265–302), 15 classroom studies, N = 827: corrective feedback had significant, durable effects. "Effects were larger for prompts than recasts and most apparent in measures that elicit free constructed responses." Younger learners benefited more than older ones. Setting (second vs foreign language) did not matter. Long treatments beat short-to-medium ones — [Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)

### Inferences
- An AI tutor that works step by step (checking each turn, giving immediate targeted feedback) can plausibly reach a large share of what a human tutor achieves. VanLehn's finding that step-level feedback rivals human tutors is the relevant evidence.
- In lesson mode (Magda), prefer prompts ("Quasi — come si dice 'a ticket'?" or a clarification request) over plain recasts for the lesson's target. In open scene play, recasts in character keep the flow. That matches Fuori's existing rule for characters, and Lyster & Saito suggest the lesson mode can push harder.
- Adults gained less from corrective feedback than children in Lyster & Saito, which argues for adding brief explicit focus for adult learners (consistent with Norris & Ortega).

### Gaps
- Li (2010) and Brown (2016) corrective-feedback meta-analyses and the Bloom (1984) original were not fetched. Rich descriptive studies of what expert tutors do (e.g., Lepper's INSPIRE model, Chi's interactive tutoring) were not retrieved.
- No tutoring meta-analysis specific to one-to-one adult L2 speaking was found.
