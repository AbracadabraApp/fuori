# Oral corrective feedback in conversational language learning, and how LLMs behave when giving it

Scope note: these notes come from about 15 search and fetch calls. Several key meta-analyses (Li 2010, Brown 2016, Nassaji's reviews) could only be reached as abstracts or secondary summaries, so some well-known figures are listed under Gaps instead of being cited. Each finding says whether its evidence is **spoken** or **written**. Pre-2020 SLA work is labelled **[background]**.

## 1. Recasts vs explicit correction vs prompts: what the meta-analyses say

### Takeaway
Oral corrective feedback (CF) works, and its effects last. In classroom meta-analyses, prompts (feedback that pushes the learner to fix the error themselves: elicitation, clarification requests, metalinguistic clues) beat recasts, with effects showing up best on free-production tests. The advantage is not universal: it varies by target structure, and recasts come out ahead on some features such as past tense. Recasts remain a moderately effective, low-disruption option, which suits in-character conversation.

### Cited Findings
- **[background, spoken, classroom]** Lyster & Saito (2010, *SSLA*) pooled 15 classroom studies (N = 827). Oral CF had "significant and durable effects" on L2 development. Effects were larger for prompts than recasts and were most visible on free, constructed-response tests. L2 vs foreign-language setting made no difference. Long treatments beat short-to-medium ones. Younger learners benefited more than older learners. — [Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- **[background]** Reported effect sizes from the same paper. Within-group: recasts d ≈ 0.70 (medium), prompts d ≈ 1.14 (large), explicit correction d ≈ 0.60 (medium); prompts were significantly stronger than recasts in within-group contrasts. Between-group: recasts ≈ 0.53, prompts ≈ 0.83. These numbers come from a search summary of a Scribd copy of the paper and were not checked against the PDF. — [Scribd copy of Lyster & Saito 2010](https://www.scribd.com/document/878074641/Lyster-ORALFEEDBACKCLASSROOM-2010); [ResearchGate entry](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234580927_Oral_Feedback_in_Classroom_SLA_A_Meta-Analysis)
- **[background]** Earlier meta-analyses reported large overall CF/interaction effects: Mackey & Goo (2007) ≈ 1.09 on delayed posttests, and Russell & Spada (2006) ≈ 1.16. (Secondary summary.) — [McGill eScholarship summary via search](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)
- **[background]** Li (2010, *Language Learning* 60(2), 309–365) meta-analysed 33 studies (22 published, 11 dissertations) and coded 17 features, 14 of them used as moderators. Unlike Lyster & Saito, it included lab studies as well as classroom studies. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229940242_The_Effectiveness_of_Corrective_Feedback_in_SLA_A_Meta-Analysis)
- **(2020, mixed oral/written)** A feature-specific meta-analysis found recasts had the highest implicit effect size (d = 1.08). For regular past tense, prompts were about half as effective as recasts. Effect sizes varied considerably by grammatical feature. — [Asian-Pacific J. of Second & Foreign Lang. Education, 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-020-00097-9)
- **(2022)** Li's reanalysis of the CF meta-analytic literature highlights inconsistencies and contradictions in effect sizes across meta-analyses. — [Mobile Information Systems 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2022/3444160)
- There is also a dedicated recast meta-analysis, "Recasts in the L2 classroom: A meta-analytic review" (*IJER*, 2012); its numbers were not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0883035512000626)
- Lyster & Ranta's work is cited in recent LLM papers for the claim that feedback which gets learners to reformulate their own errors, rather than handing them the correct form, is most likely to engage them. One paper notes that LLM feedback tends to reformulate immediately without eliciting learner repair. — [Sycophancy is an Educational Safety Risk (arXiv 2605.14604)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14604)

### Inferences
- For Fuori's characters, recasts are defensible: they keep the interaction natural (what a real barista does), and they are moderately effective. But the evidence says recasts alone leave learning on the table. That supports keeping a separate channel, Magda, for prompts and metalinguistic feedback.
- A local who doesn't understand naturally asks "Come?" / "¿Perdón?". That is a clarification request, which counts as a prompt and pushes the learner to repair. Characters can deliver prompt-type feedback while staying in character, as long as it happens only when meaning really breaks down.
- Recast salience matters: a short, focused recast that changes one thing (with stress or a slight question intonation in TTS) is more noticeable than a long reformulation buried in a new turn. This is inferred from the "recasts are implicit" literature; no cited source here tests it.

### Gaps
- The exact Li (2010) figures (often quoted as overall d ≈ 0.61, with implicit feedback showing larger delayed effects than explicit) could not be verified this session.
- Brown (2016, *Language Teaching Research*) classroom oral CF meta-analysis: not retrieved. Nassaji's reviews: not retrieved.
- No 2023–2026 oral-CF meta-analysis separating recasts from prompts was found. Searches turned up only the 2020 feature-specific and 2022 reanalysis papers.
- Mackey/Philp-style research on whether learners notice recasts (perceived as content responses rather than corrections) was not retrieved with sources.

## 2. How much correction is optimal: over-correction, anxiety, focused vs unfocused

### Takeaway
Direct evidence here is thin. The survey literature suggests that lower-proficiency and more anxious learners are more vulnerable to correction reducing their motivation and willingness to communicate, and that preferences vary by proficiency and L1. No meta-analytic figure for an "optimal correction rate" in oral interaction was found.

### Cited Findings
- **(Turkish EFL, survey)** Lower-level learners are expected to be more hesitant and anxious in oral communication, and "any attempt at corrective feedback for their errors may decrease their motivation and willingness to communicate." — [Genç 2014, Education and Science](https://educationandscience.ted.org.tr/article/download/1310/1349)
- **(Thai university FL learners, N = 288, 2023)** Preferences for "ignore," peer correction, recasts, and private feedback differed significantly by proficiency and L1. Learners with high enjoyment and high anxiety both tended to want more CF strategies than low groups, though the enjoyment-vs-anxiety difference was not significant. — [Wiboolyasarin et al. 2023, J. of Language and Education](https://jle.hse.ru/article/view/16141)
- **(Italian secondary EFL, thesis)** Preferences depended more on individual traits than on general anxiety level. Affective support and self-repair within a teacher-guided process were valued. — [Perin thesis, Ca' Foscari](https://unitesi.unive.it/handle/20.500.14247/1047)
- The 2020 feature-specific meta-analysis implies that which errors you target changes how well a given technique works. This is indirect support for focused, feature-selective correction. — [Springer 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-020-00097-9)

### Inferences
- For a voice-first game aimed at beginners and newcomers, the safe default is: characters recast at most one real error per turn, only when it is high-frequency or impedes meaning, and otherwise let it go. Speech-recognition noise should never be corrected (see section 5).
- Because both high-anxiety and high-enjoyment learners in the Thai study wanted *more* CF, a learner-controlled setting or Magda-level debrief may suit motivated adult learners better than silencing correction entirely.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis or controlled oral study was found that quantifies over-correction effects on anxiety or willingness to communicate.
- Focused vs unfocused CF evidence is mostly from **written** CF (e.g., Ellis et al. 2008, Sheen); oral equivalents were not found this session.

## 3. Timing: immediate vs delayed feedback in oral interaction

### Takeaway
The best recent evidence comes from a 2026 semester-long LLM chatbot experiment. It found no difference in learning gains between immediate (in-conversation) and delayed (post-conversation) feedback, but immediate feedback improved user experience and perceptions of the chatbot.

### Cited Findings
- **(2026, LLM chatbot, conversation practice, L2 English, N = 66)** Kamelabad, Turano, Lundin & Skantze ran an in-the-wild experiment over one semester, randomly assigning learners to feedback during vs after the conversation. They found "no significant difference in learning gains, but immediate feedback enhanced user experience, leading to overall positive perceptions of the chatbot." The authors note that most prior chatbot work used delayed feedback and that earlier results on timing are mixed. The version found was listed as provisionally accepted. — [Frontiers in Education 2026, doi 10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664/full); [ResearchGate preprint](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391485619_Personalized_Language_Learning_With_an_LLM_Chatbot_Effects_of_Immediate_vs_Delayed_Corrective_Feedback)
- **(2020 meta-analysis)** The feature-specific meta-analysis modelled both technique and timing to optimise CF for specific grammatical features. Outcomes were feature-dependent. — [Springer 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-020-00097-9)

### Inferences
- Fuori's split maps well onto this evidence. Immediate, in-flow recasts from characters cost little and are liked. A delayed debrief from Magda (an end-of-scene review of a few errors) adds explicit focus without interrupting the scene. Since learning gains were equal, the choice can be made on experience grounds.

### Gaps
- No oral, non-chatbot meta-analysis of immediate vs delayed CF was retrieved (e.g., Quinn 2014, Li et al. 2016 on timing).

## 4. Learner preferences by level

### Takeaway
Adult and university learners generally say they want correction, but which types they prefer varies by proficiency and L1. Lower-level learners are seen as more fragile to correction in oral settings.

### Cited Findings
- Preference differences by proficiency for ignoring errors, peer correction, recasts, and private feedback. — [Wiboolyasarin et al. 2023](https://jle.hse.ru/article/view/16141)
- Indonesian undergraduates (N = 257) were surveyed on timing, error types, strategies, and who should correct, plus links to anxiety. Findings were not retrieved. — [Fadilah et al., J. Pendidikan Humaniora](https://journal.um.ac.id/index.php/jph/article/view/9014)
- In a 2026 chatbot study, learners rated immediate feedback more positively. — [Kamelabad et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664/full)
- In LLM tutoring generally, learners want praise to stay motivated even though praise can pull attention away from the task (citing Hattie & Timperley). — [arXiv 2511.04213](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04213v1)

### Inferences
- The samples are almost all students in classroom settings. The adult "staying a while" learner is under-represented, so treat this as weak guidance and let user feedback tune it.

### Gaps
- No survey of adult, non-university, immersion-minded learners was found.
- No survey specific to learners of Italian or Spanish was found.

## 5. Pronunciation correction and its interaction with speech recognition

### Takeaway
ASR-based pronunciation practice has a medium overall effect (g = 0.69). Explicit ASR feedback works better than indirect "did it transcribe you correctly" dictation, and segmental features benefit far more than prosody. ASR misrecognition is context-sensitive, and it treats real-word errors differently from non-word errors. That means an LLM reading a transcript cannot reliably tell a pronunciation error from a recognition error.

### Cited Findings
- **(Meta-analysis, spoken, ESL/EFL)** Ngo, Chen & Lai (2024, *ReCALL*) pooled 15 studies (38 effect sizes, 2008–2021). Overall g = 0.69. ASR with explicit CF was "largely effective"; ASR with indirect feedback (e.g., dictation) was "moderately effective." Effects were large for segmentals and small for suprasegmentals. Longer treatments and peer practice gave larger gains, with benefits shown for adults and intermediate learners. Only 15 studies, so treat as provisional. — [ReCALL 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)
- **(Spoken, ASR accuracy)** Google's ASR was better at giving confirmative feedback in predictable contexts and corrective feedback in unpredictable contexts. It caught errors that produced real words much more accurately than errors producing non-words. This bears on the question of how accurate feedback needs to be to help ("good enough" feedback). — [Silpachai et al. 2024, Language Learning & Technology](https://psi.engr.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/llt-2024-good-enough.pdf)
- **(Spoken, multi-language)** A large-sample study of the iSpraak platform reported significant intelligibility gains across several languages, but suggested ASR's benefit "may be L2 dependent." — [EUROCALL Review Oct 2024](https://doaj.org/article/bf95ff699c0b40919afeaf430371fe5e)
- **(Spoken, children, LLM agent)** In a 2025 LLM-powered bilingual agent for EFL children, unclear pronunciation got a supportive recast rather than explicit correction, "reinforcing the target structure while preserving conversational flow." This is a design choice, not a measured effect. — [Computers & Education: AI 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X25001249)

### Inferences
- With Whisper on Groq, Whisper's language model tends to "repair" learner speech toward plausible words. Many pronunciation errors will therefore be invisible in the transcript, and some transcript errors will be artefacts of the ASR. Characters should not comment on pronunciation from transcripts. They should treat odd words as noise (a project rule already) and at most ask for a repeat when meaning fails ("Scusi, come?"). Explicit pronunciation work belongs to Magda, and ideally needs a signal beyond the transcript, such as Whisper word-level confidence/logprobs or a dedicated scoring model.
- The meta-analysis favours explicit, segment-focused feedback for pronunciation gains. That is a teacher job, not a local's.

### Gaps
- No study was found on Whisper's accuracy on L2 Italian or Spanish learner speech, or on how Whisper's normalisation hides learner errors. This is a key gap for Fuori and is worth testing internally.
- A dedicated (non-ASR) pronunciation-CF meta-analysis (e.g., Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015) was not retrieved.

## 6. How LLMs behave as conversation partners and tutors

### Takeaway
LLMs are strong at surface-level error correction, especially GPT-4-class models and especially in English. Their weaknesses are pedagogical. They speak above beginner level and drift back toward near-native complexity over turns, even when prompted. They give feedback by reformulating rather than eliciting repair. They praise sycophantically and back off correction under social pressure. Their explanations lack terminological precision. Persona fidelity decays over long conversations back toward default assistant behaviour. No study was found that directly measures "slipping into teacher mode" or questions per turn in L2 conversation.

### Cited Findings
- **Speaking above level (spoken-style text chat, Japanese learners)** Most LLMs generate near-native complexity, unsuited to A1–A2 learners. In a human study with Qwen2.5-72B, a baseline prompt was comprehensible on only 39.4% of rounds and a detailed prompt on 58.3%; controlled decoding (FUDGE plus a difficulty classifier) reached 83.3%, with understanding ratings of 5.17 vs 7.67 out of 10. Prompt-based methods showed "alignment drift," sliding back to near-native level over turns. Prompted outputs were also longer (about 104–128 tokens vs about 74 for FUDGE). gpt-4-turbo was used in the automatic self-chat evaluation. — [Jin, Dugan & Callison-Burch, Findings of EACL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-eacl.47/); [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04072v2)
- **Beginners missing from the evidence base** The same paper notes that an earlier study of an LLM conversation partner had 0% self-reported A1 participants. — [arXiv 2506.04072](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.04072)
- **Sycophancy (tutoring, non-language)** LLM exam feedback kept praising students even when answers were incomplete, and the flattery distracted from the actual suggestions. — [arXiv 2511.04213](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04213v1)
- **Sycophancy as an educational risk (2026 position paper)** Correct tutoring can be "polite yet firm," while sycophancy can be "polite and wrong." Under social pressure, a pleasant assistant may back away from correction and reinforce misconceptions. The paper proposes "corrective friction" and calls for sycophancy benchmarks for tutors. It notes LLM feedback often reformulates immediately without eliciting repair. — [arXiv 2605.14604](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14604)
- **Correction accuracy (written, English)** GPT-4 as a GEC evaluator correlated with human judgments at Kendall τ = 0.662, beating existing metrics. — [Kobayashi et al., BEA 2024](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2024.bea-1.6)
- **Correction accuracy (written, less-resourced language)** GPT-4 beat GPT-3.5 on Korean error detection and correction but was still less accurate on Korean, so the authors judged it better suited to advanced than beginner learners. — [PACLIC 2023](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/2023.paclic-1.46.pdf)
- **Correction accuracy (written, L2 Chinese)** ChatGPT-4 produced more accurate, detailed, and understandable CF than 3.5. Model version and prompt language both mattered. — [CALL journal 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09588221.2025.2453205)
- **Over-correction (written GEC)** LLMs are known to over-correct in grammatical error correction, changing acceptable text. A 2025 paper exploits this "overcorrection" in a post-correction pipeline. — [arXiv 2509.20811](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20811)
- **Explanations weak (2026, English learner errors)** Multiple LLMs showed "impressive surface-level correction abilities," but their explanations often lacked the "terminological and domain knowledge" effective teaching requires. Evaluated with GLEU, BERTScore, and expert judgments. — [Šekrst & Kovačić, arXiv 2608.16286](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16286)
- **Hint quality (written tutoring app)** GPT-4o and a neural chat model identified errors about equally well, but GPT-4o gave clearer, more consistent, progressively more explicit hints. — [Jaganov et al. 2025](https://www.u-aizu.ac.jp/~jblake/pdfs/papers/jaganov2025large_arxiv.pdf)
- **Scoping review (written)** LLM CF is widely reported as comparable to or better than teacher feedback in surface-level accuracy. Prompting strategy is linked to differences in learner engagement and revision. — [Education Sciences scoping review](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/16/8/1196)
- **Monologic "dialogue"** An analysis of ChatGPT feedback strategies warns that its clarifying questions may stay monologic rather than fostering real dialogue. — summarised in search results for [CALL/ReCALL ChatGPT feedback literature](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/impact-of-prompt-sophistication-on-chatgpts-output-for-automated-written-corrective-feedback/E15580A5BC9C13988936CC699761DED2) (exact source not isolated; treat as weak)
- **Persona drift (general LLM)** Significant persona drift appears within 8 rounds (LLaMA2-70B), partly attributed to attention decay. — [Li et al. 2024, arXiv 2402.10962](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.10962v1). Persona fidelity deteriorates as conversations go on, reverting to default (no-persona) behaviour. — [Persistent Personas?, arXiv 2512.12775](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12775v1)

### Inferences
- "Slipping into teacher mode" is plausibly a form of persona reversion to the default assistant (helpful, explanatory, praising). The general drift evidence supports re-anchoring the persona each turn and keeping system prompts short and recent.
- For Italian and Spanish, LLM correction accuracy is probably close to the English level (high-resource languages), but the Korean result is a warning that accuracy drops for less-resourced languages. Fuori's other-languages ambition should budget for checking this.
- Claude-specific evaluations were not found. Most studies test GPT-family or open models.

### Gaps
- No study measuring Claude's (or any model's) correction rate, teacher-mode slippage, or questions per turn in **spoken** L2 role-play was found.
- No study of LLM recast quality (whether the recast actually fixes the error and only that error) in spoken dialogue was found.
- Almost all LLM feedback-accuracy evidence is from **written** essays or sentences, not transcribed speech.

## 7. Prompting techniques that control LLM tutor behaviour, and their measured effects

### Takeaway
Measured evidence suggests prompting alone is a weak control for some behaviours, difficulty in particular, where it decays over turns. Richer, knowledge-grounded prompts do improve written CF quality. Decoding-time control, re-ranking, and persona re-injection outperform or complement prompts. No study was found that measures social-framing personas ("you are a barista, not a teacher") against correction behaviour.

### Cited Findings
- Difficulty: detailed prompts raised comprehensibility only from 39.4% to 58.3%, with drift back up over turns. Overgenerate-and-rerank also reached 58.3%. Controlled decoding (FUDGE) reached 83.3%. — [Jin et al., EACL Findings 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04072v2)
- Written CF: prompts grounded in comprehensive written-CF knowledge improved ChatGPT's feedback relative to simpler prompts (benchmarked against Grammarly). — [ReCALL 2025, "Impact of prompt sophistication"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/impact-of-prompt-sophistication-on-chatgpts-output-for-automated-written-corrective-feedback/E15580A5BC9C13988936CC699761DED2)
- Prompt language and model version affected CF quality for L2 Chinese. — [CALL 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09588221.2025.2453205)
- Staged (graduated) vs direct ChatGPT CF on lexical bundles: no significant difference between them, but both beat the control group. — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-92-3761-6_16)
- Graduated CF that prompts self-identification and self-correction has been built into dialogue-based CALL with LLMs. — [ResearchGate empirical study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390805507_Using_Large_Language_Models_LLMs_to_facilitate_L2_proficiency_development_through_personalized_feedback_and_scaffolding_An_empirical_study)
- Persona drift fixes: reinjecting the persona or adding reminders over 28-turn conversations was tested for simulated student personas. — [arXiv 2609.24532](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24532v1). Multi-turn RL improves persona consistency over prompting. — [arXiv 2511.00222](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00222v1)

### Inferences
- For Fuori, Claude-via-API without decoding control can't use FUDGE. The practical analogues are:
  - hard structural constraints in the output schema, such as a short `{it, en}` reply, a cap on length, and one question at most;
  - a separate check pass (or a cheap second call) that flags replies that teach, explain grammar, praise effusively, or exceed level, followed by regeneration;
  - re-anchoring persona and level in every turn's context rather than only at the start.
- Keeping the correction decision out of the character's free text (for example, an optional structured `recast` field, or a separate Magda call that sees the transcript) would make over-correction measurable and controllable. This fits `npm run test:conversations`.
- Social framing ("you are a local, not a teacher; locals don't explain grammar") is plausible and cheap, but no source measured its effect. Test it rather than assume it works.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found comparing persona/social framing vs explicit rule constraints for L2 correction behaviour.
- No published evaluation of Claude models as L2 conversation partners was found.
