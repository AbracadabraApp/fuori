# Learning a Language Through Spoken Conversation with AI/LLM Partners: SLA Evidence (2023-2026)

Scope note: Almost all the empirical work is on **English as a foreign language (EFL)**, mostly university students in East Asia, Türkiye, Iran and the Middle East. I found no controlled study of adults learning Italian or Spanish by voice with an LLM. Several key full texts (Wang et al. 2024 RER, Lyu 2025 IJAL, the 2025 *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications* study, the *System* WTC studies) were paywalled (HTTP 403). Figures for those come from abstracts or search snippets and are flagged. Quality flags: [S] = small sample (<60), [Short] = 8 weeks or less, [NoCtl] = no control group, [Self] = self-report outcome, [Pre-LLM] = rule-based or retrieval chatbot from before ChatGPT (background only).

## 1. Speaking/oral proficiency gains from conversing with LLM or chatbot partners

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses that mostly cover pre-LLM and early-LLM chatbots find **moderate positive effects**, about g = 0.48-0.61 overall, with a speaking subgroup around g = 0.6. Oddly, **voice-based chatbots show the smallest effect** (g = 0.38). LLM-era primary studies are mostly small (n of about 40-90), short (4-12 weeks), and in classrooms. They tend to find gains in grammar, vocabulary and task completion more consistently than in fluency or pronunciation.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses (mixed pre-LLM and LLM)**
- Wang, Cheung, Neitzel & Chai (2024/2025, *Review of Educational Research*): 28 studies, 70 effect sizes, robust variance estimation. Overall moderate positive effect on language learning performance, **g = 0.484**. Significant moderators were educational level, language level, interface design and **interaction capability**. Speaking-specific subgroup figures could not be verified because the full text was paywalled. — [Wang et al., RER](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00346543241255621); [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381447647_Does_Chatting_with_Chatbots_Improve_Language_Learning_Performance_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Chatbot-Assisted_Language_Learning)
- Lyu et al. (2025, *International Journal of Applied Linguistics*): 31 comparative studies, 41 effect sizes. Medium effect on L2 learning, **g = 0.608** (Z = 6.77, p < .001). Moderators examined were mobile access, chatbot modality, **whether generative AI was involved**, and the type of comparison group. A secondary source reports high heterogeneity. Moderator results were not verified (paywalled). — [Lyu et al., IJAL](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ijal.12668); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390850220_Effectiveness_of_Chatbots_in_Improving_Language_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Comparative_Studies)
- "Unraveling Effects of AI Chatbots on EFL Learners' Language Skill Development: A Meta-analysis" (about 2024): speaking subgroup from 16 studies, **g = 0.608, 95% CI [0.244, 0.972]**. **Voice-based chatbots had the smallest effect (g = 0.380, 95% CI [0.055, 0.705])** and text-based chatbots the largest. Caution: these figures come from a snippet. The identical 0.608 value appears for Lyu's overall effect, so one of the two attributions may be a search-snippet conflation. Verify against the full text. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380298384_Unraveling_Effects_of_AI_Chatbots_on_EFL_Learners'_Language_Skill_Development_A_Meta-analysis); [OUCI](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/4Eeaa0q4/)
- Liu, Zhang & Yang (2025, *Computer Assisted Language Learning*): meta-analysis of 25 EFL studies from 2022-2024, which makes it the most LLM-era-weighted synthesis. Speaking-specific results were not confirmed. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388829750_Can_AI_chatbots_effectively_improve_EFL_learners'_learning_effects-A_meta-analysis_of_empirical_research_from_2022-2024)
- Secondary citations in 2025 papers mention a meta-analysis reporting g = 0.72 (academic performance) and g = 0.77 (proficiency), and a 2024 meta-analysis of 49 studies (SMD 0.982) saying AI is most effective for listening and speaking. These are second-hand figures, unverified. — [search summary of Lyu/Wang context](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ijal.12668)

**LLM-era primary studies**
- Kang (2025, *Korean Journal of English Language and Linguistics*): 48 Korean EFL learners, ChatGPT group vs comparison group. Gains in **grammar, vocabulary and task fulfillment**, but **no significant between-group difference in fluency or pronunciation**. Whether practice was by voice is unclear from the abstract. [S] — [KASELL PDF](http://journal.kasell.or.kr/xml/46430/46430.pdf)
- ChatGPT-assisted speaking practice, complexity/accuracy/fluency (CAF) measures (2026, *Asian-Pacific Journal of Second and Foreign Language Education*, Springer): the experimental group improved more in fluency, vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation, with lower anxiety and higher motivation. Learners noted that ChatGPT "lacks emotional intelligence." Sample details were not retrieved. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-026-00452-2)
- Chinese EFL quasi-experiment (2026, *Frontiers in Education*): 83 university students, AI-supported speaking intervention, measured oral proficiency and anxiety. Details not retrieved. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1799269/full)
- Mixed-methods study (2025, *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications*): AI conversation bots vs traditional speaking practice. Reports improved speaking and anxiety reductions, especially for learners with high baseline anxiety. Full text was paywalled, so sample size and duration are unverified. — [Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05550-z)
- Kamelabad, Turano, Lundin & Skantze (2026, *Frontiers in Education*): 66 enrolled and **54 analyzed** Czech university EFL learners (self-rated mostly B1-B2), 12 sessions over 4 weeks, GPT-3 (text-davinci-003), **typed rather than voice**, immediate vs delayed corrective feedback. **No significant grammar learning difference** (d = 0.37, p = .092). The immediate-feedback group rated the bot as more effective (d = 0.56, p = .0495). 72% said they learned from it. There was no pre/post proficiency test. [S][Short][Self] — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664/full)
- Fathi, Rahimi & Derakhshan (2024, *System* 121, 103254): an AI-mediated speaking platform's effect on EFL fluency, lexicon, grammar, pronunciation and WTC. Results were not retrieved. — [repository listing via search](https://research.uok.ac.ir/_Pages/Research.aspx?Id=71481)
- Self-efficacy study (ERIC EJ1446766): ten weeks of ChatGPT speaking tasks vs a control group. Speaking self-efficacy was significantly higher in the experimental group (5.76 vs 4.04). [Self] — [ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1446766.pdf)
- Iraqi EFL chatbot study (2025): significant gains in fluency and intonation, smaller gains in pronunciation, and higher self-reported WTC. — [Shanlax](https://shanlaxjournals.in/journals/index.php/english/article/view/9514)

**Background, pre-LLM**
- Çakmak (2022), Replika, 12 weeks: speaking performance improved, but L2 speaking anxiety was not reduced. [Pre-LLM] — [via ResearchGate search summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391455139_A_comparison_of_the_effects_of_AI-based_chatbots_and_peer_interactions_on_speaking_anxiety_among_EFL_learners)

### Inferences
- Effect sizes of about 0.4-0.6 are typical of educational-technology interventions measured on researcher-made tests over short periods. Expect a shrink toward the lower end for real-world, unsupervised, long-term use.
- The lower effect for voice bots in the pre-LLM literature probably reflects older speech recognition and rigid dialogue, not voice as such. Whisper-class speech recognition plus LLM dialogue is a different system, but **no meta-analysis yet isolates LLM-plus-voice**.
- The pattern "grammar, vocabulary and task gains, but not fluency or pronunciation" (Kang 2025) suggests that short exposure builds explicit knowledge and confidence before automatized fluency. Fluency likely needs much more time on task.

### Gaps
- No verified speaking-only effect size for LLM-era voice partners. No meta-analysis restricted to post-2023 generative-AI voice conversation.
- No controlled study in Italian or Spanish as target languages, or with adult residents abroad rather than students.
- Few studies use standardized oral proficiency tests (OPI, CEFR-rated speaking). Most use researcher-made rubrics.

## 2. Speaking anxiety and willingness to communicate (WTC): AI vs human partner

### Takeaway
Most 2024-2026 studies find **lower speaking anxiety and higher WTC with AI partners** than with peers or human examiners. Effects are small to moderate (e.g., d = 0.39), and the comparisons often confound partner type with format. Learners still **prefer humans for empathy**. A pre-LLM 12-week study found no reduction in anxiety, and novelty is a recurring confound.

### Cited Findings
- Susoy (2026, *Frontiers in Psychology*): within-subjects crossover, **48** Turkish ELT undergraduates, a GPT-4-turbo plus Whisper speaking exam vs a human examiner. Pre-exam anxiety was lower before the AI exam (FLSAS 98.5 vs 102.9; t(47) = 2.67, p = .01, **d = 0.39**). The anxiety-achievement correlation was **significant with the human examiner (r = -.50) and near zero with the AI (r = -.04)**. Scores did not differ (d = 0.13). **10 of 11 interviewees preferred the human examiner for empathy** despite higher anxiety. Weaknesses: the paper reports internal statistical inconsistencies between text and tables, names novelty as a confound, and is a single session. [S][Short] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832806/)
- Ballıdağ & Aydın (2025): structured chatbot dialogues produced **significantly lower speaking anxiety** than peer-only interaction among EFL learners. Sample not retrieved. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391455139_A_comparison_of_the_effects_of_AI-based_chatbots_and_peer_interactions_on_speaking_anxiety_among_EFL_learners)
- *System* (2025), "The impact of AI chatbots on EFL learners' oral proficiency and willingness to communicate": the human-AI group showed **higher WTC, lower anxiety and greater confidence than the human-human group** (from abstract snippet; full text paywalled). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X2500329X)
- 2025 HSSC mixed-methods study: anxiety reductions were **largest for learners with high baseline anxiety**. The comparator was traditional practice, not a matched human interlocutor. — [Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05550-z)
- Qualitative study of ChatGPT's **voice mode** (8 EFL freshmen): speaking with the bot felt less intimidating than with classmates or lecturers, with less fear of judgment. [S][NoCtl] — [ELTIN Journal](https://e-journal.stkipsiliwangi.ac.id/index.php/eltin/article/download/7154/2470)
- Idiodynamic study of Chinese EFL learners speaking with AI chatbots (2026, *System*): tracked moment-to-moment enjoyment, anxiety and breakdown fluency. It shows that anxiety fluctuates within AI conversations rather than disappearing. Detailed findings not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X26001661)
- Survey and scenario study (2026, *Behavioral Sciences*): examined whether AI-assisted practice transfers to readiness for **human-directed** communication (anxiety, self-efficacy, evaluation pressure). Findings not retrieved. It is relevant because the question of transfer from AI comfort to talking with humans is open. — [MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16101770)
- Counter-evidence: Çakmak (2022), Replika, 12 weeks, improved speaking but **did not reduce anxiety**. [Pre-LLM] — [via ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391455139_A_comparison_of_the_effects_of_AI-based_chatbots_and_peer_interactions_on_speaking_anxiety_among_EFL_learners)
- "From Novelty to Strategy" (about 2025-26): novelty was reported by all learners, but it was intertwined with appreciation for the AI's accessibility and **low-anxiety practice**. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/401566847_From_Novelty_to_Strategy_The_Trajectory_of_Learner_Perceptions_in_Human_and_AI-Assisted_L2_Speaking_Tasks)

### Inferences
- The mechanism the evidence supports is **less fear of negative evaluation**: the AI doesn't judge, so anxiety stops suppressing performance (Susoy's vanishing correlation). For Fuori, this argues for keeping characters warm and non-judgmental and recasting rather than flagging errors. That matches the existing design.
- Learners' preference for human empathy suggests the persona layer matters. Characters who remember the learner and show warmth may capture some of what learners miss in "neutral" bots. This is untested.
- Whether low-anxiety AI practice transfers to WTC with real Italians or Mexicans is the key open question for a learner living abroad.

### Gaps
- No study compares an LLM partner playing an **in-world character with memory** against a generic tutor bot on anxiety or WTC.
- Few longitudinal anxiety measures. Most are single-session or pre/post over a few weeks.

## 3. Input, Output and Interaction Hypotheses applied to AI partners

### Takeaway
The theory maps cleanly. AI supplies unlimited input (Krashen), forces pushed output (Swain), and can give interactional feedback such as recasts and clarification requests (Long). But the **empirical evidence that chatbots genuinely negotiate meaning is thin and mostly pre-LLM**. LLMs' default problem is the reverse of what Krashen requires: their **default output is near-native and incomprehensible to beginners**, and prompting alone may not fix it.

### Cited Findings
- Yin & Satar (2020, *Journal of Educational Technology & Society*/LLT-style CALL study): EFL learners with a pedagogical bot (Tutor Mike) and a conversational bot (Mitsuku) produced **26 and 42 negotiation-for-meaning routines** respectively. Chatbots did create negotiation opportunities. [Pre-LLM][S] — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1250583.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340492284_English_as_a_foreign_language_learner_interaction_with_chatbots_Negotiation_for_meaning)
- AI pedagogical chatbots as L2 conversational agents (2024, *Cogent Education*): framed in interactionist theory, describes dialogs offering "extended engagement in attending to linguistic forms, refining output, and negotiating meaning." Qualitative. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2331186X.2024.2327789)
- Kamelabad et al. (2026): an LLM chatbot designed around Long's Interaction Hypothesis, but it **did not code or measure negotiation of meaning**. Its feedback consisted of recasts and explicit correction. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664/full)
- Background: interaction meta-analyses (Keck et al. 2006; Mackey & Goo 2007) find interaction benefits L2 acquisition (cited in search summaries of reviews). — [Academia.edu review](https://www.academia.edu/16404697/The_Role_of_Negotiation_of_Meaning_in_L2_Interactions_An_Analysis_from_the_Perspective_of_Long_s_Interaction_Hypothesis); [ResearchGate: Interaction and ISLA](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325629431_Interaction_and_instructed_second_language_acquisition)
- **Comprehensibility problem:** Jin, Dugan & Callison-Burch (EACL 2026): most LLMs produce text "at a near-native level of complexity," unsuitable for CEFR A1-A2. **"Prompting alone does not work."** Controllable generation raised beginner comprehensibility **from 39.4% to 83.3%** in a user study with university learners of Japanese. They propose Token Miss Rate (share of incomprehensible tokens), which correlates strongly with human judgment. — [arXiv 2506.04072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04072)
- Partly conflicting: Malik et al., "From Tarzan to Tolkien" (CaLM, ACL Findings 2024) also notes that GPT-4 defaults to native-level text, but found **GPT-4 controls CEFR level well with few-shot prompting**, with more elaborate prompts improving control at token cost. Their fine-tuned open model beat GPT-4. — [arXiv 2406.03030](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.03030v1)
- ELLMA-T (2024, social VR, GPT-4): GPT-4 estimated learner level fairly accurately and adapted its vocabulary, yet **learners at C1 said later conversations were sometimes too difficult for beginners**. — [arXiv 2410.02406](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02406)

### Inferences
- The discrepancy between Jin et al. and CaLM likely reflects target level (A1-A2 vs a broad CEFR range), language (Japanese vs English), and metric (per-token comprehension vs level classification). For Fuori's beginners, assume that **prompting gets you partway** and measure comprehensibility explicitly. One way is a Token-Miss-Rate-style check against the learner's known vocabulary.
- An LLM is good at the "modified output" side of interaction (recasts, rephrasing on request), but by default it rarely *signals non-understanding*. It tends to guess the meaning charitably, especially when instructed to forgive speech-recognition noise. That removes the "pushed output" pressure that negotiation creates. A design choice exists here: occasional in-character clarification requests ("Come, scusa?") when meaning is genuinely ambiguous would restore Swain/Long-style pressure.
- Speech-recognition noise complicates negotiation. Errors from Whisper and learner errors are confounded, so the character can't always know whether to recast.

### Gaps
- **No LLM-era study coding negotiation-of-meaning episodes** (clarification requests, confirmation checks, comprehension checks) in spoken learner-LLM dialogue was found.
- No evidence on whether learners notice LLM recasts in voice mode, where there is no visible text to compare against.

## 4. Immersion-style learning (doing tasks in the language) vs instruction, especially adults abroad

### Takeaway
Study-abroad research shows that residence in the country reliably improves **oral fluency** more than staying at home, with smaller gains in accuracy. The length-of-stay effect is inconsistent across meta-analyses. Immersion alone is not sufficient: gains depend on actual interaction, which many sojourners don't get. This is the gap an AI conversation partner could fill.

### Cited Findings
- Tseng, Liu, Hsu & Chu (2024, *Language Teaching Research*), multilevel meta-analysis: study-abroad length is positively associated with gains, but extended *domestic* programs don't necessarily match them. The review reports larger effects for 11-13-week stays (d = 0.981) than for stays over 13 weeks (d = 0.458). Hirai's earlier analysis found a trend toward longer being better, so **the direction of the length effect is contested**. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1362168820988423)
- Llanes & Muñoz (2013): adult study-abroad learners gained fluency over time (**d = 0.77**). At-home learners showed no change (d = 0.10). Segalowitz & Freed (2004) similarly found study abroad helped oral fluency more than staying home (both cited in Tseng et al. and related reviews). — [SAGE (Tseng et al.)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1362168820988423)
- A synthesis of 11 quantitative studies found that sojourning facilitates global proficiency and oral fluency, while **oral and written accuracy changed less**. Reviews rate study-abroad research as moderate methodological quality. Stays of 6 weeks or less give inconclusive results. — [British Council / TeachingEnglish review](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/sites/teacheng/files/pub_h136_final_web.pdf); [Moore 2021 systematic review](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/rev3.3306)
- Fluency scoping review (*System*, 2018): synthesizes fluency development in study abroad. Fluency is the most consistently improved dimension. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X17307856)
- Implicit vs explicit instruction: single studies conflict on durability. Rezaei & Mehraein (2019) found explicit instruction ahead on tests but implicit instruction more durable. Another IJAL study found explicit instruction more durable. — [IJAL](https://ijal.khu.ac.ir/article_4579.html); [IJAL 3016](https://ijal.khu.ac.ir/article-1-3016-en.pdf)

### Inferences
- The study-abroad pattern (fluency up, accuracy flat) predicts what pure conversation practice will do. Fuori's recast-in-character feedback, plus Magda's teaching from M2 onward, is a reasonable hedge for accuracy.
- Living abroad without interaction is common. A game that rehearses the actual local encounters (station, bar, market) could act as a bridge that raises real-world WTC. That is a hypothesis, not evidence.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the canonical meta-analyses on explicit vs implicit instruction (e.g., Norris & Ortega 2000; Spada & Tomita 2010; Kang, Sok & Han 2019 in *LTR*). The well-known finding that explicit instruction tends to produce larger measured effects on discrete-point tests is therefore not sourced here.
- No research found on **adult long-stay residents** (expats or retirees, not students) learning through AI conversation.

## 5. Known limits: novelty, short durations, learner level, AI speaking above level

### Takeaway
The strongest documented limit is the **novelty effect**: interest in chatbot partners drops after the first task, unlike interest in human partners. It only partly rebounds months later. Most studies run 4-12 weeks with small samples, so long-term gains are unknown. Learner level moderates effects, and LLMs' default output is too hard for beginners.

### Cited Findings
- Fryer, Ainley, Thompson, Gibson & Sherlock (2017, *Computers in Human Behavior*): 122 Japanese EFL students, 12-week experiment. **Task interest dropped after the first chatbot task**, a drop not seen with human partners. Human-partner task interest predicted later course interest, while chatbot task interest did not. [Pre-LLM] — [ScienceDirect](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563217303667?via=ihub%3D); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317270477_Stimulating_and_sustaining_interest_in_a_language_course_An_experimental_comparison_of_Chatbot_and_Human_task_partners)
- Fryer, Nakao & Thompson (2019, *Computers in Human Behavior*) follow-up: a **small but significant rebound** in chatbot interest five months later, not back to the initial level. The authors suggest **spaced chatbot use** may sustain interest. [Pre-LLM] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563218306095); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329749402_Chatbot_learning_partners_Connecting_learning_experiences_interest_and_competence)
- Huang, Hew & Fryer (2022, *JCAL*) systematic review: novelty is cited as a likely reason chatbot engagement and performance gains are short-term. The review calls for longer studies. — [Wiley AM PDF](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/am-pdf/10.1111/jcal.12610); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354813647_Chatbots_for_language_learning-Are_they_really_useful_A_systematic_review_of_chatbot-supported_language_learning)
- Learner level and education level significantly moderate chatbot effects (Wang et al. 2024 meta-analysis; direction not verified). — [RER](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00346543241255621)
- Durations in LLM-era studies reviewed here: Kamelabad et al. 4 weeks (with 18% attrition), the ERIC self-efficacy study 10 weeks, Susoy single sessions, Fryer 12 weeks. — sources above
- Novelty is flagged as a confound in LLM-era work (Susoy 2026: participants had no prior chatbot experience). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832806/)
- LLM technical issues reported by learners: incomplete sentences, repeated questions, bugs (Kamelabad et al. 2026). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664/full)
- Concerns raised across reviews: feedback accuracy, lack of emotional responsiveness, over-reliance. A systematic review of chatbots and speaking proficiency lists engagement and practice-opportunity benefits alongside "lack of accuracy" limits. — [JREALL review](https://riset.unisma.ac.id/index.php/JREALL/article/view/23866); [Springer 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-026-00452-2)
- AI speaking above learner level: see Section 3 (Jin et al. 2026; CaLM 2024; ELLMA-T 2024). — [arXiv 2506.04072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04072)

### Inferences
- The novelty finding comes from rigid pre-LLM bots in compulsory courses. Open-ended LLM characters with memory and a changing world may decay less, but nobody has tested that. Fuori's daily "change of scenery" and recurring characters are plausible countermeasures. The Fryer rebound finding supports **spaced, daily-session pacing over binge use**.
- Because most evidence is from 4-12-week classroom studies with EFL university students, any claim that "AI conversation builds speaking proficiency" for a self-directed adult learner of Italian over months is an extrapolation.
- Beginners are the riskiest group: they need the most comprehensible input, LLMs serve them worst by default, and the anxiety benefit matters most for them.

### Gaps
- No LLM-era longitudinal study (over 6 months) of conversational AI and speaking proficiency.
- No study of novelty decay with LLM voice partners specifically.
- No retrieved evidence on outcomes broken down by beginner vs intermediate for LLM-era voice partners, beyond the moderator listing in Wang et al.
