# Grammar Terms, Explained Through English

Source: Sergio Adorni and Karen Primorac, *English Grammar for Students of Italian*, 2nd ed. (Olivia and Hill Press, 1995), Josh's copy; all 47 chapters, summarised in our own words with our own examples.

The book explains each term in English first, then shows where Italian differs. This file keeps that order. Each point is placed at the level where a learner first meets it, using `functions-grammar.md` (Proudfoot's CEFR levels) and the Progetto summaries (`a1-progetto-1a.md`, `a2-progetto-1b.md`, `b1-b2-progetto-2.md`). Where the two disagree, both are noted. Each entry gives the English idea, then the Italian difference and the trap, then one example pair (English | Italian).

## A1: the first weeks

### Parts of speech, function and agreement
*A1 · the book's introduction*
- **In English:** Words belong to classes (noun, verb, adjective, pronoun…), and each one does a job in the sentence (subject, object). Most English words keep the same form whatever job they do.
- **In Italian:** The classes and jobs are the same, but most words change form to match the noun they go with: the article, the adjective and the verb all agree with it. One English word can need different Italian words for different jobs ("that" can be *quel*, *che* or *quello che*). **Trap:** translating word for word, idioms included ("take a walk" is *fare una passeggiata*).
- **Example:** The little white cups are on the counter. | Le tazzine bianche sono sul bancone.

### Nouns and gender
*A1 · Progetto 1a intro*
- **In English:** Only people and animals have gender (he, she). Everything else is "it".
- **In Italian:** Every noun, even a table or an idea, is masculine or feminine, and that gender sets the form of its article, adjectives and pronouns. Clues: *-o* is usually masculine, *-a* usually feminine, *-e* can be either; borrowed words ending in a consonant are masculine (*il bar*, *lo sport*); *-zione*, *-tà* and *-trice* are feminine. Exceptions exist (*la mano*, *il problema*). **Trap:** guessing gender from meaning. Learn every noun with its article.
- **Example:** The station is far; the museum is close. | La stazione è lontana; il museo è vicino.

### Plurals
*A1 · Progetto 1a intro*
- **In English:** You add *-s* (or change the word: man, men).
- **In Italian:** The final vowel changes: *-o* and *-e* become *-i*, *-a* becomes *-e*. Words ending in an accented vowel or a consonant don't change (*il caffè, i caffè*; *la città, le città*; *il bar, i bar*). A few switch gender in the plural (*l'uovo, le uova*). **Trap:** adding *-s*, or forgetting that the article and adjective change too.
- **Example:** two coffees and two croissants | due caffè e due cornetti

### Indefinite articles (a, an)
*A1 · Progetto 1a Unit 1*
- **In English:** *A* or *an*, picked by the sound that follows.
- **In Italian:** Four forms, picked by gender and first sound: *un* (most masculine nouns), *uno* (masculine before *z*, *s* + consonant, *gn*, *ps*), *una* (feminine), *un'* (feminine before a vowel). There is no plural "a"; Italian uses the partitive (*dei*) or nothing. **Trap:** the apostrophe goes only on the feminine: *un amico* but *un'amica*.
- **Example:** a student and a (female) friend | uno studente e un'amica

### Definite articles (the)
*A1 · Progetto 1a intro*
- **In English:** One word, *the*, for everything.
- **In Italian:** Seven forms by gender, number and first sound: *il, lo, l', la* (singular), *i, gli, le* (plural). Italian also uses "the" where English doesn't: for general ideas, countries, titles when talking about someone (*la signora Bianchi*), before possessives (*la mia borsa*), and with a weekday to mean "every" (*il sabato*). **Trap:** leaving the article out the English way.
- **Example:** Coffee is good for you. | Il caffè fa bene.

### Descriptive adjectives
*A1 · Progetto 1a intro and Unit 1*
- **In English:** Adjectives never change, and they come before the noun.
- **In Italian:** They agree in gender and number (*-o, -a, -i, -e*; adjectives in *-e* only change for the plural, to *-i*). Most follow the noun; a handful of common ones usually come before it (*buono, bello, grande, piccolo, nuovo*). When English uses a noun as an adjective ("orange juice", "coffee cup"), Italian puts the describing noun after a preposition: *succo d'arancia*, *tazza da caffè*. **Trap:** forgetting agreement, or stacking nouns English-style.
- **Example:** a red wine and two cold beers | un vino rosso e due birre fredde

### Subject pronouns (I, you, he…)
*A1 · Progetto 1a intro*
- **In English:** Every verb needs a stated subject, including "it" and "they" for things.
- **In Italian:** *io, tu, lui, lei, Lei, noi, voi, loro* exist, but are usually dropped because the verb ending already shows who. They are used for contrast or to make clear who is meant. "It" and "they" for things are almost never said. **Trap:** saying *io* in every sentence (it sounds insistent), or hunting for a word for "it".
- **Example:** It's closed. They're open on Sundays. | È chiuso. Sono aperti la domenica.

### Familiar and formal "you"
*A1 · Progetto 1a Unit 1*
- **In English:** One *you* for everyone, one person or many.
- **In Italian:** *tu* for one person you're on first-name terms with, *voi* for several, *Lei* for one person you'd address with a title or don't know. *Lei* takes the he/she form of the verb. The book's formal plural *Loro* is now rare; *voi* covers any group. The choice also changes pronouns and possessives (*ti / La*, *tuo / Suo*). **Trap:** mixing *tu* and *Lei* in one conversation. When unsure, start with *Lei* and let the other person offer *tu*.
- **Example:** Where are you from? (friend / stranger) | Di dove sei? / Di dov'è?

### Infinitive
*A1 · Progetto 1a Unit 2*
- **In English:** *To* + verb ("to eat"), or the bare verb after *can* or *must*.
- **In Italian:** One word ending in *-are*, *-ere* or *-ire*; the ending tells you the verb's group. It follows any conjugated verb, modals included (*posso pagare*, *devo andare*). **Trap:** English phrasal verbs ("turn on", "look for") are separate verbs in Italian (*accendere*, *cercare*). Look up the whole phrase; don't add a preposition to the base verb.
- **Example:** Can I pay by card? | Posso pagare con la carta?

### Conjugation: stem and ending
*A1 · Progetto 1a Unit 1*
- **In English:** A verb barely changes in the present (it only adds *-s* for he/she/it).
- **In Italian:** Every tense has six forms. Drop *-are/-ere/-ire* to get the stem, then add the ending for the person. Regular verbs follow one of three patterns; irregular ones (*essere, avere, andare, fare, stare*) must be memorised. Some *-ire* verbs add *-isc-* (*capisco*). **Trap:** the *lui/lei* form also serves for formal *Lei*; and using the infinitive as a verb ("io parlare").
- **Example:** We speak a little Italian. | Parliamo un po' d'italiano.

### Present tense
*A1 · Progetto 1a Unit 1*
- **In English:** Three presents: "I drink", "I'm drinking", "I do drink".
- **In Italian:** One present (*bevo*) covers all three. It also covers plans ("I'm leaving tomorrow": *domani parto*) and, with *da*, things that started in the past and are still going, where English uses "have been". **Trap:** reaching for a past tense for "I've lived here for…".
- **Example:** I've been living in Rome for two months. | Abito a Roma da due mesi.

### Present progressive (stare + gerundio)
*A1 as a chunk (Proudfoot); Progetto teaches it in B1*
- **In English:** *Be* + *-ing* is used constantly: right now, this semester, next week.
- **In Italian:** *Stare* + *gerundio* (*-ando*, *-endo*) exists, but only for what is happening at this moment. Habits, the current period and plans use the plain present. **Trap:** overusing *sto facendo*.
- **Example:** What are you doing this summer? / What are you doing right now? | Cosa fai quest'estate? / Cosa stai facendo adesso?

### "-ing" as a noun (the English gerund)
*A1 (Mi piace + infinitive)*
- **In English:** An *-ing* word can act as a noun: "Cooking relaxes me", "before leaving".
- **In Italian:** That job goes to the infinitive: *mi piace cucinare*, *prima di uscire*. **Trap:** using the *-ando* form as a noun.
- **Example:** I love walking around Trastevere. | Mi piace camminare per Trastevere.

### Helping verbs (do, will, would, used to)
*A1 concept*
- **In English:** *Have, be* and *do* help other verbs, and so do *will, would, used to, may*.
- **In Italian:** Only *avere* and *essere* help, to build compound tenses. "Do", "will", "would" and "used to" are not separate words; the verb ending carries them (*parlerò, parlerei, parlavo*). **Trap:** looking for a word for "do" or "will".
- **Example:** Did you sleep well? | Hai dormito bene?

### Negative sentences
*A1 · Progetto 1a intro*
- **In English:** *Not* after a helping verb, or *do not* + verb.
- **In Italian:** Put *non* right before the verb; no "do". Object pronouns sit between *non* and the verb (*non lo so*). **Trap:** putting *non* after the verb, or translating "don't".
- **Example:** I don't understand. | Non capisco.

### Questions and tag questions
*A1 · Progetto 1a intro*
- **In English:** Add *do/does/did*, or swap subject and verb. The tag matches the verb: "isn't it?", "don't you?"
- **In Italian:** Keep the statement's word order and raise your voice at the end; a named subject can move to the end (*È arrivato Marco?*). The tag never changes: *vero?* or *no?* **Trap:** looking for "do"; building a tag from the verb.
- **Example:** Do you take milk? It's good, isn't it? | Prendi il latte? È buono, vero?

### "To be" that isn't essere
*A1 · Progetto 1a Units 3 and 5*
- **In English:** *To be* covers existence, age, hunger, weather and health.
- **In Italian:** *Essere* covers most of it, but: *c'è / ci sono* say something exists or is somewhere; *ecco* points it out ("here it is", no verb); *avere* covers sensations and age (*ho fame, sete, freddo, sonno, fretta*; *ho trent'anni*); *fare* covers weather (*fa caldo*); *stare* covers health (*sto bene*). **Trap:** *sono freddo* describes a cold person; feeling cold is *ho freddo*.
- **Example:** It's cold and I'm hungry. Is there a pharmacy nearby? There it is! | Fa freddo e ho fame. C'è una farmacia qui vicino? Eccola!

### Possession with di (no 's)
*A1*
- **In English:** "Marco's sister" or "the sister of Marco".
- **In Italian:** Only the "of" pattern: thing + *di* + owner, with *di* fusing with the article (*del, della, dei…*). **Trap:** the word order flips from English.
- **Example:** Marco's sister; the barista's cat | la sorella di Marco; il gatto del barista

### Possessive adjectives (my, your, his…)
*A1 for mio/tuo/suo (Progetto 1a Unit 3); full set A2 (1B Unit 6)*
- **In English:** The word matches the owner (his, her) and never changes with the thing.
- **In Italian:** It matches the thing owned in gender and number, usually with the article (*la mia borsa, i miei amici*). *Suo* means his, her, its, or formal your; *loro* never changes. With one family member, drop the article (*mia madre*), except with *loro* and in the plural (*i miei fratelli*). **Trap:** *suo/sua* follows the thing, not the owner, so it doesn't tell you whose it is.
- **Example:** her brother; his sister | suo fratello; sua sorella

### Prepositions
*A1 · Progetto 1a Units 1–3*
- **In English:** *In, on, at, to, for…*, which can also dangle at the end ("Who are you going with?").
- **In Italian:** *di, a, da, in, con, su, per, tra/fra*, and five of them fuse with the article (*al, nel, dal, sul, del*). They don't map one to one: "to Rome" is *a Roma*, "to Italy" *in Italia*, "to the doctor's" *dal medico*. Some English verbs that take a preposition take none in Italian (*aspettare, cercare, guardare, pagare*: wait for, look for, look at, pay for), and the reverse (*entrare in*, *telefonare a*). A preposition never dangles; it goes first. **Trap:** translating the preposition literally.
- **Example:** Who are you going with? I'm going to Marco's. | Con chi vai? Vado da Marco.

### Partitive (some, any)
*A1 form (Progetto 1a Unit 3); confident use A2*
- **In English:** *Some* or *any*, often left out.
- **In Italian:** *di* + article: *del pane, dell'acqua* (things you can't count, singular), *dei pomodori, delle olive* (things you count, plural). *Un po' di* is the easy alternative. It's optional in questions and dropped in negatives (*non ho pane*). **Trap:** using it after *non*.
- **Example:** I'd like some bread and some olives. | Vorrei del pane e delle olive.

### Question words (who, what, which, how much)
*A1*
- **In English:** *Who/whom, whose, what, which, how much/many*; prepositions often dangle at the end.
- **In Italian:** *chi* (who and whom), *di chi* (whose: "of whom is it?"), *che / cosa / che cosa* (what), *quale/quali* (which, a choice; *qual è*), *quanto/a/i/e* (agrees with the noun). "What is…?" asking for a definition is *cos'è*; asking which one is *qual è* (*Qual è il tuo numero?*). A preposition goes first: *A chi…? Con chi…?* **Trap:** *cosa* where a choice is meant.
- **Example:** Whose umbrella is this? How many coffees? | Di chi è questo ombrello? Quanti caffè?

### Demonstratives (this, that)
*A1 for questo; quello's forms A2 (1B Unit 6)*
- **In English:** *This/these* near you, *that/those* farther away.
- **In Italian:** *questo, -a, -i, -e* near; *quello* far. Before a noun, *quello* copies the definite article: *quel, quello, quell', quella, quei, quegli, quelle*. Alone, as a pronoun, it is simply *quello, quella, quelli, quelle*. **Trap:** *quello* before a noun changes like *il/lo/l'*.
- **Example:** This one or that one? That cake there. | Questo o quello? Quella torta lì.

### Adverbs versus adjectives
*A1*
- **In English:** Adverbs describe verbs and adjectives; casual English often uses adjectives instead ("drive slow"). *Good* is an adjective, *well* an adverb.
- **In Italian:** Adverbs never change; adjectives agree. *Buono* describes a thing, *bene* describes how. *Molto* is an adverb (unchanged) before an adjective or after a verb, and an adjective (agreeing) before a noun. **Trap:** *sto buono*, or *molti stanchi*.
- **Example:** The coffee is good and I feel well. A lot of people, very kind. | Il caffè è buono e sto bene. Molta gente, molto gentile.

### Conjunctions
*A1 for e, ma, perché, quando; B1 for those that take the subjunctive*
- **In English:** Words that join: *and, but, because, although, before, after*. *Before* and *after* work as both prepositions and conjunctions.
- **In Italian:** Conjunctions never change, but one English word may need two Italian ones: before a noun, "before" is *prima di*; before a clause it is *prima che*, which takes the subjunctive. *Perché* means "because", "why" and "so that". **Trap:** using *prima di* with a full clause.
- **Example:** before dinner / before he leaves | prima di cena / prima che parta

### Future tense
*A1 end (Progetto 1a Unit 5); A2 (Proudfoot)*
- **In English:** *Will* + verb, or "going to", or the present progressive.
- **In Italian:** One word: a stem from the infinitive + endings (*parlerò, partirò*; irregular stems like *andrò, sarò, avrò*). In speech the present often covers plans (*stasera esco*). "Going to" has no Italian equivalent with *andare*. After *quando* or *appena* about the future, Italian uses the future where English uses the present. **Trap:** *vado a mangiare* means "I'm off to eat", not a future.
- **Example:** When I get to Rome, I'll call you. | Quando arriverò a Roma, ti chiamerò.

## A2: the past, pronouns, commands and politeness

### Tense and mood
*A2 concept*
- **In English:** Tense says when; mood says how the speaker treats the action: fact, command or wish. English has few one-word tenses.
- **In Italian:** Four moods (indicative, imperative, subjunctive, conditional) and more one-word tenses: present, *imperfetto*, *passato remoto* (the written or southern simple past, *andò*, B1 for recognition only) and future. **Trap:** English tenses don't map one to one; "I studied" can be three different Italian tenses.
- **Example:** I studied (once / as a habit / in a history book) | ho studiato / studiavo / studiai

### Past participle
*A2 (Progetto 1a Unit 4)*
- **In English:** The form after "have" (*eaten, spoken*), also used as an adjective (*a broken cup*).
- **In Italian:** *-ato, -uto, -ito*, with many irregulars (*fatto, preso, visto, detto, scritto, aperto, chiuso*). As an adjective it agrees with the noun. **Trap:** irregular participles have to be memorised.
- **Example:** The shop is closed; the door is open. | Il negozio è chiuso; la porta è aperta.

### Passato prossimo
*A2 (Proudfoot); Progetto teaches it halfway through A1*
- **In English:** A finished past event is either "I ate" or "I have eaten".
- **In Italian:** One tense covers both: *avere* or *essere* + past participle. Most verbs take *avere*. Verbs of movement or change of state, plus all reflexives, take *essere*: *andare, venire, arrivare, partire, entrare, uscire, nascere, morire, restare, stare, diventare, piacere, succedere*. With *essere* the participle agrees with the subject (*Anna è arrivata*); with *avere* it agrees only with a *lo/la/li/le* placed before it (*l'ho vista*). **Trap:** "I have" isn't always *avere*: "I've gone" is *sono andato/andata*.
- **Example:** We went to the market and bought cheese. | Siamo andati al mercato e abbiamo comprato il formaggio.

### Imperfetto versus passato prossimo
*A2 (1B Unit 7); choosing confidently B1*
- **In English:** One simple past serves for both events and background. "Used to" and "was -ing" are the only clues.
- **In Italian:** The *imperfetto* tells what used to happen, what was going on, and what things were like (weather, age, time, feelings). The *passato prossimo* tells what happened. A story mixes them: background in the *imperfetto*, events in the *passato prossimo*. **Trap:** "would" meaning "used to" ("we would walk every evening") is *imperfetto*, not the conditional.
- **Example:** I was reading the paper when Marta came in. | Leggevo il giornale quando è entrata Marta.

### Past perfect (trapassato prossimo)
*A2 (1B Unit 7); B1 (Proudfoot)*
- **In English:** *Had* + participle, for the earlier of two past actions, often skipped when the order is obvious.
- **In Italian:** The *imperfetto* of *avere/essere* + participle (*avevo mangiato, ero uscita*), same auxiliary and agreement rules as the *passato prossimo*. Italian keeps the order of tenses more strictly, so use it even where English allows a simple past. **Trap:** following English and using the *passato prossimo* for both actions.
- **Example:** I told him I had already paid. | Gli ho detto che avevo già pagato.

### Objects: direct, indirect, after a preposition
*A2 concept, needed for pronouns*
- **In English:** A direct object answers "what/whom?", an indirect one "to/for whom?" ("to" is often hidden: "give her the key"), and the rest follow other prepositions.
- **In Italian:** An indirect object noun always has *a* in front, and the direct object usually comes first. Verbs often take a different kind of object than in English: *aspettare, cercare, ascoltare* take a direct object; *telefonare a* an indirect one; *ricordarsi di* and *fidarsi di* a preposition. With *piacere*, the English subject becomes the indirect object. **Trap:** copying the English pattern leads to the wrong pronoun later.
- **Example:** I'm waiting for the bus; I'll call my mother. | Aspetto l'autobus; telefono a mia madre.

### Direct object pronouns (me, him, it, them)
*A2 (1B Unit 8)*
- **In English:** *Me, you, him, her, it, us, them*, placed after the verb, in the same form for every kind of object.
- **In Italian:** *mi, ti, lo, la, La, ci, vi, li, le*, placed before the conjugated verb (attached to an infinitive or a command). "It" and "them" take the gender of the noun: *lo/la*, *li/le*. In compound tenses the participle agrees. **Trap:** putting the pronoun after the verb, or using *lo* for a feminine thing.
- **Example:** The pizza? I'll take it. The tickets? I bought them. | La pizza? La prendo. I biglietti? Li ho comprati.

### Indirect object pronouns and piacere
*mi piace A1 as a chunk; full set A2 (1B Unit 10)*
- **In English:** *(To) me, him, her, them*; "I like it" has "I" as the subject.
- **In Italian:** *mi, ti, gli, le, Le, ci, vi, gli*, before the verb. The book gives *loro* after the verb for "to them"; in speech today it's *gli*. *Piacere* means "to be pleasing to", so the thing liked is the subject: *mi piace* for one thing, *mi piacciono* for several. *Mancare, servire* and *dispiacere* work the same way. **Trap:** *io piaccio la pizza*; and *mi manchi* means "I miss you", the reverse of the English order.
- **Example:** I like this café; do you like olives? | Mi piace questo bar; ti piacciono le olive?

### Stressed pronouns (con me, a lui)
*A2*
- **In English:** The same object pronoun, stressed with the voice.
- **In Italian:** After a preposition use *me, te, lui, lei, Lei, noi, voi, loro* (*con me, per te, da lui*). The same forms go after the verb for contrast (*a me piace, a lui no*). For a thing after a preposition, Italian repeats the noun rather than saying "on it" or "near them". **Trap:** *con mi*.
- **Example:** Are you coming with me? I like it; he doesn't. | Vieni con me? A me piace, a lui no.

### Possessive pronouns (mine, yours)
*A2 (1B grammar appendix)*
- **In English:** *Mine, yours, his, hers* change with the owner, never with the thing.
- **In Italian:** The same forms as the possessive adjectives, with the article (*il mio, la tua, i suoi*), agreeing with the thing replaced. After *essere* the article is usually dropped (*è mio*). **Trap:** "hers", when it means her brother, is *il suo*.
- **Example:** Is this umbrella yours? Yes, it's mine. | Questo ombrello è tuo? Sì, è mio.

### Demonstrative pronouns ("this one", "the one that", "Marco's")
*A2*
- **In English:** *This one, that one, the one that…*, and *Marco's* standing alone.
- **In Italian:** *Questo* or *quello* alone, agreeing with the noun replaced. "The one(s) that" is *quello/quella/quelli/quelle che*. Since Italian has no *'s*, "Marco's" alone becomes "the one of Marco": *quello di Marco*. **Trap:** looking for an Italian *'s*.
- **Example:** Which coffee? The one with milk. Mine or Marco's? | Quale caffè? Quello con il latte. Il mio o quello di Marco?

### Reflexive verbs
*A2 (1B Unit 9); daily-routine chunks A1*
- **In English:** A *-self* pronoun, often left out ("I shaved", "I got dressed").
- **In Italian:** The pronoun (*mi, ti, si, ci, vi, si*) is required and goes before the verb; the infinitive ends in *-si* (*alzarsi*). Many everyday verbs are reflexive where English isn't: *alzarsi, vestirsi, sedersi, divertirsi, chiamarsi, sentirsi, ricordarsi*. The same forms express "each other" (*ci vediamo*). Compound tenses take *essere*, with agreement. **Trap:** dropping the pronoun (*alzo* means "I lift something"), or using *avere* in the past.
- **Example:** I got up early and had a great time. | Mi sono alzato presto e mi sono divertito.

### Impersonal si ("you", "one", "people")
*A2 (1B Unit 9); chunks like "Come si dice?" from A1*
- **In English:** A passive or a vague subject: "Tickets are sold here", "you can't park here".
- **In Italian:** *si* + third-person verb, plural when what follows is plural (*si vende*, *si vendono*). It's everywhere in signs and questions (*Si può…?*), and Italian prefers it to the passive. **Trap:** making the verb agree with an imagined "you".
- **Example:** Where do you buy bus tickets? | Dove si comprano i biglietti dell'autobus?

### Imperative (commands)
*A2 for tu/noi/voi (1B Unit 10); Lei form B2 (Progetto 2 Unit 6), though "Scusi!" and "Senta!" are day-one chunks*
- **In English:** The bare verb for "you" ("Turn left"), "let's" for "we", "don't" for the negative.
- **In Italian:** The form depends on who: *tu* (*gira, prendi, senti*), *noi* (*andiamo* = let's go, no word for "let's"), *voi* (*venite*). The negative *tu* command is *non* + infinitive (*non toccare!*). The *Lei* command borrows the present subjunctive (*scusi, senta, giri, prenda*). Pronouns attach to *tu/noi/voi* commands (*dimmi, prendilo*) but go before the *Lei* form (*mi dica*). **Trap:** *scusa* is for a friend, *scusi* for a stranger.
- **Example:** Turn left, then go straight (friend / stranger). | Gira a sinistra, poi va' dritto. / Giri a sinistra, poi vada dritto.

### Conditional (would)
*vorrei A1 as a fixed phrase; the tense A2 (1B Unit 11)*
- **In English:** *Would* + verb, for polite requests, wishes and results of unreal conditions.
- **In Italian:** One word: the future stem + *-ei, -esti, -ebbe, -emmo, -este, -ebbero* (*vorrei, potrebbe, dovresti*). Uses: polite requests, wishes, advice, the result half of an unreal "if", and unconfirmed news (*sarebbe chiuso*, "apparently it's closed"). **Trap:** "would" meaning "used to" is the *imperfetto*.
- **Example:** Could you speak more slowly? | Potrebbe parlare più lentamente?

### Positive and negative indefinites (someone, nothing, never)
*A2*
- **In English:** Only one negative per clause: "I don't see anyone", never "I don't see nobody".
- **In Italian:** The double negative is required: *non* + verb + *niente, nessuno, mai, da nessuna parte*. If the negative word comes first, drop *non* (*nessuno risponde*). Positive forms: *qualcosa, qualcuno, qualche* and *ogni* (both with a singular noun), *tutti, sempre*. **Trap:** *non ho qualcosa*; *qualche giorni* (it's *qualche giorno*).
- **Example:** I haven't eaten anything. | Non ho mangiato niente.

### Adverbs in -mente
*A2 (Proudfoot); Progetto treats word formation at B2*
- **In English:** *-ly* added to an adjective.
- **In Italian:** *-mente* added to the feminine form (*lenta → lentamente*); adjectives in *-le* or *-re* drop the *-e* (*facilmente, regolarmente*). Adverbs never agree. Many common ones are irregular (*bene, male, piano, presto*). **Trap:** *lentomente*.
- **Example:** She speaks slowly and clearly. | Parla lentamente e chiaramente.

## B1: guesses, regrets, comparing, joining sentences, the subjunctive

### Future perfect and the future of probability
*Progetto meets both at the end of A1; Proudfoot rates them B1*
- **In English:** *Will have* + participle; guesses use "must", "probably", "I wonder".
- **In Italian:** The *futuro anteriore* (*avrò finito, sarà partita*) is used mostly after *quando, appena, dopo che* for something finished before a future action, where English uses the present perfect. The plain future also makes guesses about now: *Sarà stanco* ("he must be tired"), *Che ore saranno?* **Trap:** reading a guess as a prediction.
- **Example:** Where's Luca? He's probably still at work. | Dov'è Luca? Sarà ancora al lavoro.

### Past conditional and the future in the past
*Progetto 1B Unit 11 (A2); Proudfoot B1*
- **In English:** *Would have* + participle for regrets; "She said she would call" for a future seen from the past.
- **In Italian:** The conditional of *avere/essere* + participle (*avrei chiamato, sarei venuto*), for regrets and advice about the past (*avrei dovuto prenotare*). It is also the future in the past, where English uses the plain "would". **Trap:** *ha detto che chiamerebbe* is wrong; Italian needs the past conditional.
- **Example:** She said she would call. | Ha detto che avrebbe chiamato.

### Comparisons and superlatives
*B1 (Progetto 2 Unit 3); più … di in chunks from A2*
- **In English:** *-er / more / less … than*; *as … as*; *the -est / the most … in*; *very*.
- **In Italian:** *più / meno* + adjective + *di* (*che* when comparing two adjectives, verbs or phrases). Equal: *(tanto) … quanto* or *(così) … come*. The superlative is article + *più* + adjective + *di*, so "in the neighbourhood" becomes "of the neighbourhood". "Very" can be *-issimo* (*buonissimo*). Irregular forms: *migliore, peggiore* (adjectives), *meglio, peggio* (adverbs). **Trap:** translating "in" after a superlative, or "than" as *che* every time.
- **Example:** This café is cheaper than that one; it's the best in the neighbourhood. | Questo bar è più economico di quello; è il migliore del quartiere.

### Relative pronouns (who, which, that, whose)
*che in chunks A1–A2; the full system B1 (Progetto 2 Unit 2)*
- **In English:** *Who, whom, which, that*, often left out ("the man I met"), with prepositions dangling at the end.
- **In Italian:** *Che* for subjects and direct objects, people or things, and it can never be dropped. After a preposition, use *cui* (*con cui, di cui, a cui*). "Whose" is article + *cui* + noun (*la cui casa*). "What" in a non-question is *quello che* or *ciò che*; "which" referring to a whole idea is *il che*; *chi* means "whoever, those who". **Trap:** dropping *che*, or ending the clause with a preposition.
- **Example:** The friend I'm having dinner with is from Naples. | L'amico con cui vado a cena è napoletano.

### Subjunctive (congiuntivo)
*B1 (Progetto 2 Unit 5; Proudfoot); confident choice B2*
- **In English:** Rare and hard to spot: "if I were", "I insist that he be".
- **In Italian:** Common, with four tenses. It appears in *che* clauses after verbs of opinion, wish, hope, emotion or doubt (*penso che, voglio che, spero che, ho paura che*), after impersonal phrases (*è possibile che, bisogna che, sembra che*), after certain conjunctions (*benché, perché* "so that", *prima che, a meno che, purché*) and in wishes with *magari*. English uses the indicative, the future or an infinitive in those places ("I want you to come": *voglio che tu venga*). With the same subject in both halves, use *di* + infinitive (*penso di restare*). **Trap:** following English; statements of certainty (*so che, è vero che*) stay indicative.
- **Example:** I hope the bakery is still open. | Spero che il forno sia ancora aperto.

## B2: hypotheticals, the passive, reported speech, -ing phrases

### Hypothetical sentences (if-clauses)
*real conditions A2; unreal present B1 (Proudfoot), Progetto 2 Unit 8; unreal past B2*
- **In English:** Real: *if* + present, *will*. Unreal now: *if* + past, *would*. Unreal past: *if* + *had* + participle, *would have*.
- **In Italian:** Real: *se* + present (or future), present (or future). Unreal now: *se* + imperfect subjunctive, conditional. Unreal past: *se* + pluperfect subjunctive, past conditional. **Trap:** never put the conditional after *se* (*se avrei* is the classic mistake).
- **Example:** If I had more time, I'd stay another month. | Se avessi più tempo, resterei un altro mese.

### Passive voice
*B1 (Proudfoot); B2 (Progetto 2 Unit 9)*
- **In English:** *Be* + past participle, with the doer after *by*; very common.
- **In Italian:** *Essere* + participle, agreeing with the subject, with the doer after *da*. *Venire* can replace *essere* for actions, and *andare* + participle means "must be" (*va timbrato*, "must be stamped"). Italian uses the passive less than English; it prefers the active or *si*. **Trap:** translating "by" as *per*.
- **Example:** The church was built in the 1600s. | La chiesa è stata costruita nel Seicento.

### Reported speech
*B2 (Progetto 2 Unit 10)*
- **In English:** Tenses shift back: "I'm tired" becomes "she said she was tired".
- **In Italian:** They shift too, more strictly: present → *imperfetto*, *passato prossimo* → *trapassato*, future → past conditional. Commands become *di* + infinitive (*mi ha detto di aspettare*). The book covers only the future in the past; the full set of shifts comes from Progetto 2. **Trap:** the future in the past (see the past conditional above).
- **Example:** "I'm tired." → She said she was tired. | "Sono stanca." → Ha detto che era stanca.

### Participles and gerundio phrases
*B2 (Progetto 2 Unit 11)*
- **In English:** An *-ing* adjective ("an interesting street"), a phrase ("Walking home, I saw…"), "by doing".
- **In Italian:** The present participle (*-ante, -ente*) is used mostly as an adjective or noun (*interessante, il cantante*). The *gerundio* (*-ando, -endo*) does the "while/by doing" job, with no preposition in front. **Trap:** adding *per* or *da* before the *gerundio*, or using it as a noun (that's the infinitive).
- **Example:** You learn by making mistakes. | Si impara sbagliando.

## Where the book is dated

Published in 1995; a few points should be taught the modern way:
- "To them" is *gli* in speech today (*gli ho scritto*), not *loro* after the verb.
- The formal plural *Loro* (and the *Loro* commands) is rare; use *voi* for any group.
- "What is…?" for a definition is *cos'è*; the book's *che è* sounds stiff.
- The book's *veduto* is now almost always *visto*.

## For Learn

The questions a beginner is most likely to ask in the first month, with the one-line answer the teacher should give before any example or table.

| Learner asks | Teacher's one-line answer |
|---|---|
| How do I know if a word is masculine or feminine? | Words ending in *-o* are usually masculine and in *-a* usually feminine; *-e* can be either, so learn every word with its *il* or *la*. |
| Why are there so many words for "the"? | "The" changes with the noun's gender, number and first sound: *il, lo, l', la* for one, *i, gli, le* for more than one. |
| When do I say *tu* and when *Lei*? | *Lei* for strangers, shopkeepers and anyone you'd call Mr or Mrs; *tu* for friends and children; if in doubt, use *Lei* and let them offer *tu*. |
| Why don't Italians say *io* all the time? | The verb ending already says who (*parlo* can only be "I speak"), so *io* is added only for emphasis or contrast. |
| How do I say "I'm hungry", "I'm 30" or "It's cold"? | Hunger, thirst, cold and age use *avere* (*ho fame, ho trent'anni*), weather uses *fare* (*fa freddo*), and "there is" is *c'è*. |
| How do I ask a question or say "I don't"? | There's no "do": for a question keep the sentence and raise your voice; for "don't", put *non* in front of the verb. |
| Why *mi piace* and not *io piaccio*? | *Piacere* means "to be pleasing to": the thing you like is the subject, so *mi piace* for one thing, *mi piacciono* for several. |
| Why *vorrei* and not *voglio*? | *Vorrei* is "I would like", the polite way to ask; *voglio* ("I want") sounds blunt when ordering. |
| Is *sto mangiando* the same as "I'm eating"? | Only for what is happening right now; for habits, this week or plans, use the plain present: *mangio*, *domani parto*. |
| How do I say what I did yesterday? | *Ho* + a verb ending in *-ato, -uto, -ito* (*ho mangiato*); verbs of going and arriving take *sono* and match you (*sono andato*, *sono andata*). |
