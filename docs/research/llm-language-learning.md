# Locals build confidence; Magda must build accuracy

The research supports Fuori's core split, with one big warning attached. Talking by voice with an AI partner reliably **lowers speaking anxiety and raises willingness to speak**. It produces **moderate, short-term proficiency gains (meta-analytic g ≈ 0.48–0.61)**, and these show up more in grammar, vocabulary and task completion than in fluency or pronunciation. Recasts from in-character locals are a defensible, low-disruption kind of feedback. Classroom meta-analyses find that **prompts, which push the learner to repair their own error, teach more than recasts**, so a separate teacher who elicits repair (Magda) is the right complement. The warning concerns the speech pipeline. Whisper's language model tends to turn a beginner's broken Italian into fluent Italian, and it **invents text on silence**. A transcript-based system will under-detect real errors, occasionally invent turns, and cannot judge pronunciation. That limits what the characters can recast and what the study file can honestly record. Three problems are well documented: LLMs speak above beginner level and drift back up even when prompted; they praise sycophantically; and they slip out of persona over long chats. Each calls for structural controls rather than longer prompts. No study covers adults learning Italian or Spanish by voice with an LLM, no study runs longer than about a semester, and no competitor has tested character memory or in-character recasting. Fuori's main bets are therefore plausible, untested inferences, and they should be instrumented from day one.

## AI partners lower anxiety more reliably than they raise proficiency

The best quantitative picture comes from meta-analyses that mix pre-LLM and early-LLM chatbots. Wang and colleagues pooled 28 studies and found an overall **g = 0.484**, with "interaction capability" among the significant moderators ([Wang et al., RER](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00346543241255621)). Lyu and colleagues pooled 31 comparative studies and reported **g = 0.608** ([Lyu et al., IJAL](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ijal.12668)). One EFL meta-analysis reportedly found **voice-based chatbots had the smallest effect (g = 0.38)** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380298384_Unraveling_Effects_of_AI_Chatbots_on_EFL_Learners'_Language_Skill_Development_A_Meta-analysis)). That figure comes from a search snippet that may be conflated with Lyu's, and it almost certainly reflects older speech recognition and rigid dialogue rather than voice as such. No meta-analysis yet isolates LLM-plus-Whisper-class voice conversation. LLM-era primary studies are small and short. A typical one is Kang's 48 Korean learners, who gained in **grammar, vocabulary and task fulfilment but showed no between-group difference in fluency or pronunciation** ([Kang 2025](http://journal.kasell.or.kr/xml/46430/46430.pdf)). Short exposure builds explicit knowledge and confidence before it builds automatized fluency.

The affective evidence is more consistent. In a within-subjects crossover with 48 Turkish undergraduates, anxiety was lower before a GPT-4 plus Whisper speaking exam than before a human one (**d = 0.39**). The anxiety–score correlation was **r = −.50 with the human examiner and r = −.04 with the AI**: the AI removed fear of evaluation as a drag on performance. Yet **10 of 11 interviewees still preferred the human for empathy** ([Susoy 2026, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832806/)). A 2025 mixed-methods study found the anxiety reduction **largest for learners with high baseline anxiety** ([Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05550-z)). The record is not uniform. A 15-week study of 90 Japanese undergraduates using ChatGPT voice found **no significant change in anxiety** ([Waseda](https://www.wrs.waseda.jp/sp-r/admin/print/number/2025C-786)). A pre-LLM 12-week Replika study improved speaking without reducing anxiety ([summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391455139_A_comparison_of_the_effects_of_AI-based_chatbots_and_peer_interactions_on_speaking_anxiety_among_EFL_learners)). Nobody has tested whether low-anxiety practice with AI carries over to willingness to talk to real Italians or Mexicans, and that is the question that matters most for a learner who lives there.

Theory maps neatly onto AI partners. Krashen's input hypothesis says learners need lots of language they can understand, Swain's output hypothesis says they need to be pushed to speak, and Long's interaction hypothesis says they learn from the back-and-forth of conversation, including feedback. An AI partner can supply all three, but the default LLM breaks the first. Jin, Dugan and Callison-Burch found most LLMs produce **near-native complexity unsuitable for A1–A2**. With a baseline prompt, beginners understood only **39.4%** of rounds, and a detailed prompt raised that to just **58.3%**. Controlled decoding reached **83.3%**, and prompt-based control showed "alignment drift" back toward native level over turns ([Jin et al., EACL Findings 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04072v2)). The CaLM work found GPT-4 controls CEFR level reasonably well with few-shot prompts ([Malik et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.03030v1)). The two results probably differ because of target level and metric: per-token comprehension at A1 is harsher than level classification. Fuori's M1 learners sit where the problem is worst. Negotiation of meaning means the clarification requests and comprehension checks that push learners to rephrase. Chatbots produced it in pre-LLM studies ([Yin & Satar 2020](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1250583.pdf)), but no LLM-era study codes it in spoken learner dialogue. An LLM told to forgive ASR noise will rarely signal non-understanding. That removes pushed-output pressure unless the design adds it back on purpose.

Novelty is the documented long-run risk. In Fryer's 12-week study, **interest in chatbot tasks dropped after the first task**, which did not happen with human partners. Interest rebounded only partly five months later, and the authors suggest that spaced use sustains it ([Fryer et al. 2017](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563217303667?via=ihub%3D); [Fryer et al. 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563218306095)). Those were rigid bots in compulsory courses. Fuori's characters with memory and a changing world could plausibly decay more slowly, but nobody has measured that. Study-abroad research gives a useful prior for what conversation alone does. Residence abroad improves **oral fluency far more than accuracy**: Llanes and Muñoz found fluency d = 0.77 abroad against 0.10 at home, and overall gains are largest for **lower-proficiency learners who combine formal courses with homestays** (g = 0.87) ([Tseng et al. 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1362168820988423); [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/revisiting-the-effectiveness-of-study-abroad-language-programs-a--2/)). Fuori's fiction of locals plus a teacher is the digital analogue of that combination.

## Recasts keep the scene alive, but prompts and explicit focus teach more

A recast is when the listener repeats what the learner said with the error fixed and carries on, without pointing out the mistake. Lyster and Saito pooled 15 classroom studies (N = 827). Oral corrective feedback had "significant and durable effects," and **effects were larger for prompts than recasts, most visible on free-production measures**. Longer treatments beat short ones, and **younger learners benefited more than older ones** ([Lyster & Saito 2010](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)). Reported within-group effects of roughly **recasts d ≈ 0.70 against prompts d ≈ 1.14** come from an unchecked copy of the paper ([Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/878074641/Lyster-ORALFEEDBACKCLASSROOM-2010)). The advantage depends on the structure being taught. A 2020 feature-specific meta-analysis found recasts strongest overall for implicit outcomes (d = 1.08), and found prompts **only about half as effective as recasts for regular past tense** ([Springer 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-020-00097-9)). So recasts are a legitimate, moderately effective tool that suits a barista or a ticket clerk. They leave learning on the table, especially for adults, and older meta-analyses favouring explicit instruction point the same way: Norris and Ortega report d = 1.13 for explicit and 0.54 for implicit, though those figures are second-hand ([Columbia SALT](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434)). Fuori already gives the explicit and prompt channel to Magda. One prompt type fits naturally in character: the clarification request ("Come, scusi?"), as long as it is used only when meaning actually breaks down.

Timing evidence favours Fuori's split. In a semester-long LLM chatbot experiment, feedback during the conversation and feedback after it produced **no significant difference in learning gains**, but immediate feedback improved user experience and perceptions of the bot ([Kamelabad et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664/full)). Since learning came out equal, the choice can rest on experience: light recasts in the flow from locals, and explicit work later with Magda. Nobody has measured the optimal amount of correction in speech. Survey work suggests lower-level, more anxious learners are more fragile to correction ([Genç 2014](https://educationandscience.ted.org.tr/article/download/1310/1349)). It also finds that high-enjoyment and high-anxiety learners alike asked for *more* corrective strategies, with preferences varying by proficiency and L1 ([Wiboolyasarin et al. 2023](https://jle.hse.ru/article/view/16141)). These samples are students in classrooms. The adult learner who is staying a while is unstudied.

The LLM-behaviour literature explains why "be a local, not a teacher" is hard to enforce. LLMs are strong at surface correction. Claude Sonnet 4.5 is reported to beat earlier zero-shot scores on BEA-2019 ([BEA 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.bea-1.17.pdf)). But LLMs **over-correct acceptable text** ([arXiv 2509.20811](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20811)). Their explanations lack precise terminology ([Šekrst & Kovačić](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16286)). They reformulate the learner's sentence at once instead of eliciting repair. And they back away from correction under social pressure: tutoring can be "polite yet firm," but sycophancy is "polite and wrong" ([arXiv 2605.14604](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14604)). LLM exam feedback kept praising incomplete answers, and the flattery buried the actual suggestions ([arXiv 2511.04213](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04213v1)). **Persona drift appears within about 8 rounds**, and characters revert toward default assistant behaviour as conversations lengthen ([Li et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.10962v1); [Persistent Personas?](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12775v1)). "Slipping into teacher mode" is most plausibly that reversion. The measured fixes are to re-inject the persona over long conversations ([arXiv 2609.24532](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24532v1)) and to use controls outside the prompt. No study has measured whether social framing ("you are a barista") controls correction behaviour. No study has evaluated Claude as a spoken L2 partner. No study has checked whether LLM recasts in speech fix the error, and only the error.

## Ten-minute lessons consolidate; they cannot teach to mastery

No source sets how many items fit in a short spoken lesson. The surrounding evidence is clear on direction. Learners need roughly **6–20 encounters** before a word sticks ([HK EDB summary](https://cd1.edb.hkedcity.net/cd/eng/vocab09/ch1_3-3.htm)). **Spacing beats massing**, and the best gap between sessions grows with the retention interval ([Cepeda et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). An L2-specific meta-analysis of 37 experiments confirms the spacing benefit, with effects that vary by skill and activity ([Kim & Webb 2022 via Serrano](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602)). Retrieval practice, meaning producing from memory rather than re-reading or repeating, beats restudy at **g ≈ 0.50–0.63** ([Adesope et al. 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306)) and helps L2 grammar and vocabulary specifically ([CUNY dissertation](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_etds/5083)). Together these make Magda's one-goal design correct in shape. The opening "recall" earns its place only if it is genuine retrieval: Magda sets up a situation and the learner must produce the phrase. A reminder followed by repetition does not count. The target also has to come back in later lessons and scenes, because one lesson can produce several correct uses but not learning. Interleaving, meaning mixing structures within practice, has mixed L2 grammar evidence and looks weakest for speaking ([UB thesis](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/216425/1/245169.pdf)). That argues for a drill on the single target and only light mixing with old items in the role-play.

Fuori's stopping rule (about three correct unprompted uses, shrink the goal after repeated misses, cap at about 20 replies) has **no direct validation, and nothing contradicts it either**. The popular "85% rule" comes from machine-learning models. Its author says the useful message is only that a sweet spot of difficulty exists ([Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/learning-optimized-15171/)). Spacing research implies diminishing returns from drilling longer in one sitting. The design question is therefore less "how many correct uses" and more "does the learner retrieve it tomorrow." Shrinking the goal after misses matches the desirable-difficulties framing: too hard now means simplify, and too easy means add a twist.

The lesson's structure fits evidence about task-supported teaching. TBLT (task-based language teaching) programmes show positive effects, but the original d = 0.93 was re-estimated at **g = 0.61**, and critics argue that most included programmes were really "task-supported" ([LTR reanalysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/13621688231167573); [Boers & Faez 2023](https://todoele.net/node/442136)). That describes Magda's teach-and-drill, then role-play, then post-task focus. Repeating the role-play helps. Oral task-repetition meta-analysis shows **large effects on complexity and accuracy, smaller on fluency** ([Abdi Tabari et al. 2025](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19111)). Repeating the same talk under shrinking time (4/3/2) produced lasting fluency gains ([Kwansei](https://kwansei.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/30914/files/2.pdf)). A second run of the role-play with a small variation is cheap and well supported. On transfer to the real world, the evidence is small studies. Communicative out-of-class tasks got completed more often than workbook homework ([Barrantes Torres et al. 2013](https://www.revistas.ucr.ac.cr/index.php/rlm/article/view/12609)). Study-abroad learners who consistently did "field performance" tasks joined more in local life and spoke with more confidence ([JNCOLCTL 2020](https://doaj.org/article/155531885d07456d9df128f0e2ee6dfd)). Transfer depended on how similar learners judged the outside task to what they had practised ([James 2008](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994609487360)). The "one thing to try in town" should reuse the drilled phrase in the same kind of place. The next lesson's recall should ask how it went, which turns the street attempt into retrieval.

Tutoring benchmarks set expectations. Human one-to-one tutoring is about **0.79 SD over no tutoring, not Bloom's 2 sigma**, and step-based intelligent tutors reach about 0.76 ([VanLehn via MIT slides](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf)). Real-world tutoring programmes average **0.29 SD** ([Nickow et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476)). Turn-by-turn targeted feedback is the mechanism that brings software close to human tutors, and that is exactly what Magda's drill phase does.

## Whisper hides the errors the study file wants to record

Fuori's learning notes depend on transcripts, and the evidence shows two opposite failures. The first is hiding errors. ASR systems with strong internal language models smooth learner errors toward correct forms. In a spoken grammar study, **Whisper's verbatim accuracy was 46% against 84.7% for a grammar-aware model**, and Whisper sometimes silently fixed article errors ([arXiv 2410.01579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01579)). Whisper output "tends to be grammatical" and drops fillers ([eScience Center](https://blog.esciencecenter.nl/immaculate-speech-and-hallucinations-ea87e545a9ef)). Error-preserving ASR research names the same problem: the smoothing removes exactly what corrective feedback needs ([ACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.348/)). The second failure is inventing errors, when a misrecognition gets blamed on the learner. The damage concentrates where Fuori's learners are. Knill and colleagues found pronunciation feedback for A1 learners most exposed to ASR error, and grammar feedback most variable at B1 ([Knill et al. 2018](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/152761/8/Knill%20et%20al%202018%20Impact%20of%20ASR%20performance%20on%20free%20speaking%20language%20assessment,%20ISCA.pdf)). Content-level judgements stayed robust to ASR error ([patent](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11854530)), and that matches Verhelst and Belpaeme's finding that **LLMs "cover for" speech-recognition mistakes** and keep conversations going ([imec](https://imec-publications.be/handle/20.500.12860/59084)). The upshot: meaning-level signals are trustworthy, morphology-level signals are not. English fallbacks and words not understood are robust signals. "Wrong article gender" is fragile, and "no error seen" is weak evidence that the learner is accurate.

The grammar-checking step has its own bias. GPT-4 excelled on fluency-style correction but **"significantly underperformed" on the minimal-edit BEA-2019 benchmark** ([Goto et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13624)). For L2 Italian specifically, **human annotators beat ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot and Llama 3** at error detection, with performance varying by error type ([Fioravanti et al., CLiC-it 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.clicit-1.44)). No LLM evaluation of learner Spanish error correction exists ([COWS-L2H](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.894)). A training-free fix is to sample several corrections and keep only the edits a majority agree on, which reduces over-correction across nine benchmarks ([Goto et al., BEA 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.bea-1.60/)). Proficiency estimation is similar. With calibration examples, GPT-4 rated CEFR levels almost as well as automated writing evaluation, but agreement varied by L1 ([Yancey et al. 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.bea-1.49)). Zero-shot speech scoring clustered at 7–9 out of 10 and **never gave a low score to 863 utterances that humans scored 6 or below** ([arXiv 2601.16230](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16230v1)). Prompting with the CEFR can-do descriptors is the best-supported zero-shot method and yields explanations Magda can reuse ([Bannò et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10200)). Zero-shot LLMs also find weaknesses more readily than strengths ([arXiv 2605.04298](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.04298v1)). A mistakes-only study file will skew Magda negative.

The cumulative-summary design carries a known structural risk. Recursive memory (fold each session into the previous summary) improves long-dialogue consistency, but **outdated information carried in memory degrades responses** ([LLM-Rsum](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.15022)). Current agent-memory systems such as Mem0 and Zep have moved from rewriting whole documents to storing atomic facts with explicit add, update and delete operations ([arXiv 2601.04463](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04463)). Production language apps model at the level of items. Duolingo's half-life regression, which tracks how fast each word fades from memory, cut recall-prediction error by over 45% and **raised retention in practice sessions by 9.5% in A/B tests** ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P16-1174.pdf); [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn)). Nobody has measured how much a repeatedly rewritten learner summary drifts. The predictable failure modes follow from the mechanism: quotes get paraphrased until they no longer match what was said, items vanish silently, fixed errors persist, and one slip becomes "struggles with X." A ~20-line file that an LLM rewrites cannot track counts or dates. Item-level data can.

## The speech pipeline fails on silence, waits and pronunciation

Silence is the most concrete hazard. On non-speech audio, Whisper large-v3 produced text **40.3% of the time**, and the rate rose with clip length (11.6–62.3% for 1–30 s clips). Top outputs were "thank you" (24.8%) and "thanks for watching" (10.3%). **Silero VAD before transcription cut WER on non-speech-augmented sets from 104.8 to 8.0, and VAD plus a "bag of hallucinations" filter reached 6.5**. Beam size 1 did not help at all ([Barański et al., ICASSP 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11378v1)). The artefacts depend on language: Spanish gives "Subtítulos creados por la comunidad de Amara.org" ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/subtitulos-creados-por-amara-org/1113265/2)), French gives an equivalent, and Arabic, German and Romanian have their own credit lines ([Kieran Healy](https://kieranhealy.org/blog/archives/2025/07/22/the-sound-of-silence)). Hallucinations are tied to long pauses: Careless Whisper found about 1% of transcripts contained whole invented phrases, more often for speakers with long non-vocal stretches ([Koenecke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08021v2)). Beginners pausing mid-sentence fit that profile. Groq's `verbose_json` exposes per-segment `avg_logprob`, `no_speech_prob` and `compression_ratio` but gives no cutoffs. Groq also bills a **10 s minimum per request** and rates **turbo at 12% WER against 10.3% for large-v3**, recommending large-v3 for "error-sensitive, multilingual" work ([Groq docs](https://console.groq.com/docs/speech-to-text)). Turbo behaves more like large-v2 across languages ([openai/whisper #2363](https://github.com/openai/whisper/discussions/2363)), and users report that its `no_speech_prob` looks unreliable. Zero-shot Whisper also leaves large headroom on L2 speech. Fine-tuning on Dutch cut WER **67% relative for non-native adults** ([arXiv 2502.17284](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17284v1)), and proficiency-unaware fine-tuning widened the gap that hurts low-level learners ([arXiv 2510.10738](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.10738v1)). **No WER figure exists for L2 learners speaking Italian or Spanish.**

Latency cannot match human turn-taking. Gaps between turns average about 200 ms across languages ([Levinson & Torreira 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731/full)). Industry targets of under 800 ms to 1.2 s come from vendor blogs, not studies ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/blog/voice-ai-latency-whats-fast-whats-slow-how-to-fix-it)). Older telephony work found users prefer audio waiting cues to silence even for short waits ([Fröhlich 2005](https://course.khoury.northeastern.edu/csg170/ssl/frohlich05.pdf)). Learners in a 2026 voice-chatbot study were irritated by lag, and **repeated misunderstandings made one want to quit** ([arXiv 2601.19304](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.19304)). Silence is a poor signal that a speaker has finished a turn ([Jiang et al. 2023](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2023.findings-acl.776.pdf)), which supports Fuori's tap-to-talk over automatic endpointing for learners who pause. Users accepted about 345 ms of extra latency in exchange for a more natural voice ([FH OÖ thesis](https://pure.fh-ooe.at/en/studentTheses/optimizing-multimodal-dialogue-systems-for-social-robots-in-offic/)).

Pronunciation feedback is real but needs the right tool. ASR-based pronunciation training averages **g = 0.69**. Explicit feedback is large, indirect "did it recognise you" feedback is moderate, gains on individual sounds are large and gains on prosody are small, and short training shows no advantage ([Ngo, Chen & Lai 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/effectiveness-of-automatic-speech-recognition-in-eslefl-pronunciation-a-metaanalysis/A915444CF252B61D14961D2FE733822D)). In mispronunciation detection, false alarms discourage learners, so precision should take priority ([arXiv 2209.06265](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.06265)). Whisper is not a pronunciation scorer. Its language model fills in mispronounced words, and its misrecognitions may come from noise. On speech rate, slower speech helps lower-proficiency listeners in older, mixed evidence ([Iowa State review](https://www.iastatedigitalpress.com/psllt/article/18440/galley/17405/view/)). A 2026 study found listeners *judged* globally slowed TTS clearer even though it **increased comprehension errors** relative to targeted slowing ([arXiv 2603.30032](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.30032v1)). Learners' preference for "slow" is therefore not a reliable guide.

## Competitors leave memory and in-character recasting untested

The category has converged on tutor avatars, scenarios and feedback after the chat. Duolingo Roleplay has experts write each scenario, the opening message and "where to take the conversation," and gives accuracy and complexity feedback afterwards ([OpenAI customer story](https://openai.com/index/duolingo)). Langua offers configurable real-time or no correction, error reports and drills built from frequent mistakes ([Fabryk review](https://thefabryk.com/blog/langua-review)). The user complaints line up with Fuori's design choices. Praktika users say "**no memory on common errors… in no way personalised**," and that free chat feels "more like a school teacher than a native speaker" ([Trustpilot](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/praktika.ai?page=8)). A competitor's analysis of low-star reviews of the main English apps found "**repetitive and shallow**" conversations ([Unstar 2026](https://unstar.app/blog/is-speak-elsa-talkpal-loora-learna-ai-english-apps-legit-2026)). A competitor criticised Pingo's speech recognition as "far too forgiving," accepting nonsense syllables ([LanguaTalk](https://languatalk.com/blog/pingo-ai-review/)). That last complaint is a direct warning for Fuori's forgive-the-noise rule: lenience that becomes visible erodes trust that the app is helping. Efficacy evidence is almost entirely from the companies. Duolingo's Video Call research reports gains with **no sample sizes, test names or effect sizes** ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/video-call-research-report)), and no efficacy study by Speak was found. The business signal is mixed. Max was about 5% of Duolingo's paid subscribers at the end of 2024, rising to about 9% later, and commentators expect conversation to move into cheaper tiers as costs fall ([Class Central](https://www.classcentral.com/report/duolingo-2025/)). Per-minute cost matters. No documented product combines a generated city, locals who remember the learner as a person, recasting in character and a separate teacher. That is an open gap, but no competitor's data shows these features work either.

## What to measure, and where the evidence runs out

Fuori can collect evidence that no competitor publishes. Most of it comes from logs. On **conversation quality**, run `npm run test:conversations` fixtures and sampled live transcripts. Measure comprehensibility as the share of reply tokens outside the learner's known vocabulary, a Token-Miss-Rate-style check ([Jin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04072)). Track recasts per turn and whether each recast fixed the real error and only that error, teacher-mode slips, questions per turn, praise rate, and how these drift with turn number. On the **speech pipeline**, the most valuable asset is an in-house set of recordings of Josh and testers saying scripted Italian and Spanish *errors*. That gives the normalisation rate (how often Whisper silently corrects them), WER by speaker level, hallucination rate on silence and noise clips by language and model, and p50/p90 latency from tap-release to first audio. On **study-file fidelity**, run ten synthetic sessions as a regression test: quotes still verbatim, resolved items gone, no item without a dated source. On **learning**, the best-supported proxies are delayed. They are success on Magda's opening retrieval a day or more later, unprompted use of past targets in later scenes, the trend in English fallbacks and words not understood, and the length of the learner's utterances. Add self-reported follow-through on the town task, retention at D7 and D30 to watch for novelty decay, and an occasional two-item anxiety and willingness-to-communicate check.

The evidence is thin in specific, known places. No study covers Whisper on L2 Italian or Spanish learner speech, or on how Whisper normalises errors in Romance languages. No LLM-era study runs longer than about a semester, and none covers adult residents rather than students. No study codes negotiation of meaning in spoken LLM dialogue, measures recast quality or teacher-mode slippage in speech, or evaluates Claude as a partner. No source validates a mastery criterion or an item count for short spoken lessons, or measures drift in rewritten learner summaries. No product has published credible long-term outcomes. Most effect sizes above come from EFL classrooms in East Asia, Türkiye and the Middle East, measured with researcher-made tests over 4–12 weeks. Shrink them for a self-directed adult.

## Conclusion

The literature changes where Fuori's risk sits. The pedagogy is largely sound: locals giving low-anxiety practice with recasts, and a teacher using prompts, explicit focus and spaced retrieval, is about what SLA research would design. The fragile layer is the evidence pipeline underneath. Characters can only recast what Whisper lets through, and Whisper's bias is toward fluent, correct-looking Italian. Notes can only be as true as transcripts, and LLMs over-correct and over-weight weakness. A summary rewritten every session will drift. Every downstream behaviour inherits these errors, so the highest-leverage work is less about prompt wording and more about honest signal: gate silence, grade confidence, log only what repeats, keep verbatim quotes, and judge progress by what the learner retrieves tomorrow.

The second implication concerns differentiation. Competitors are converging on the same tutor-plus-debrief formula and publishing almost no rigorous outcomes. Fuori's distinctive bets, relationship memory and in-character help, target the category's loudest complaints, but no evidence yet shows they work. Fuori's design generates the data needed to find out: transcripts, notes, delayed retrieval and town-task follow-through. Instrumenting that from the start would let Fuori become the first product in this space with a measured answer.
