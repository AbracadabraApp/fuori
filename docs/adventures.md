# Adventures

## Why

Josh: the places started out as stereotypical bakeries and gelaterias, and the Rome-style redo "had good intent but the outcome has not been strong": quaint shops, not interesting adventures. Now that the Beginner level dialogue carries the hard parts in spoken English, a beginner can follow a scene with a story in it; the language only has to carry a few words worth keeping. So the places can be more interesting.

Seen on the way (Josh's play, 2026-10-27): people placed where they have no errand get one invented. A student with a book bag at a pizza al taglio shop served Josh a sit-down pizzeria; a woman in a square tried to make him coffee. Cause: the Beginner prompt isn't told the kind of place or why the person is there, and it leads with an errand.

## Places where something is happening

Not just shops: places with a time, a crowd or a task.

- Porta Portese flea market at 7 on a Sunday: bargaining with a man selling old Lazio scarves
- The Testaccio fishmonger at dawn, who sets the best fish aside for regulars
- The anagrafe or the post office queue: a real newcomer's task (the codice fiscale, a parcel stuck in customs)
- The Curva Sud before a Roma match; a choir rehearsal in a church; a community garden; a bike repairer under a bridge

## Shops with a story or a problem

- The landlady's broken boiler, and the plumber who arrives late
- A barber who has cut the same men's hair for 40 years and knows all the gossip
- A ferramenta where you have to explain what you need without the word
- A night tram stopped by a strike, and the stranger who knows the way home

## Characters who want something from you

Someone who needs a hand, a tip, a taster for their sauce, a fourth for scopa. The learner stops being a customer and becomes part of the city.

## People with something to show (Josh)

The scholar, the priest, the docent, the professor, the journalist, the archaeologist. Each has a reason to talk and something to show, so the scene is an outing, not a transaction. In Rome, for example:

- **An archaeologist** at a dig under a church (San Clemente, three layers down) who lets you into the trench: *Guardi qui: questo è romano.*
- **A priest** at a small parish who shows you a fresco nobody visits and invites you to the Sunday festa
- **A docent** at the Capitoline who takes you past the crowd to the she-wolf and quizzes you on numbers (the year, how many twins)
- **A professor** at La Sapienza, late for class, who walks you across campus and tells you San Lorenzo's history on the way
- **A journalist** at a newsstand or press café chasing a story about the neighbourhood, who asks *you* what you've seen
- **A scholar** in a library or a reading room: a whispered scene, all short phrases

## A mystery to solve (Josh)

A thread that runs across days and places, so the city becomes a story the learner follows. One idea of the shape:

- **It starts with something small:** an old photo found in a book at the flea market, a name carved on a bench, a letter delivered to the learner's flat by mistake, a painting with a face that turns up again somewhere else.
- **The clues live with people:** the journalist knows the year, the priest has the parish register, the archaeologist recognises the building, the barber remembers the family. Each clue is an ordinary conversation at an ordinary place, and the person gives it away only if the learner asks (a question in the language: *Conosce questa persona?*, *Quando è stata fatta questa foto?*).
- **Each step is something to say:** asking, describing, telling what someone else said. That's the syllabus's own order (questions, then descriptions, then the past), so a mystery can follow the units: the learner can only ask the next question once they've learned how.
- **The learner keeps the clues:** in the Diario or Learn, with who said what; the answer is something they piece together.
- **It runs across cities (Josh):** one mystery, a chapter per city. Rome finds the photo; Naples has the family; Florence has the painter who made the portrait; Venice has the last letter. Each city adds its own people and places to the story, and the trail gives the game's change of scenery a reason (CLAUDE.md: every day comes with a change of scenery).
- **It ends somewhere:** a person found, a story told, a place opened. Then another begins.

## Geography along the way (Josh)

A mystery or an adventure can teach where things are, a little at a time:

- **The trail moves:** a clue points to another neighbourhood ("the photo was taken in Trastevere, by the river") and the learner goes there, on the city map, by tram or on foot.
- **Then to another city:** the family moved to Naples, the painter came from Siena; the trail leaves by train from the station, and the next city opens with a reason to be there.
- **Asking the way is part of it:** directions, landmarks, which tram, a ticket: the syllabus's own early units (A1 #7 directions, #13 tickets and platforms) used for something the learner wants.
- **What each place is known for:** a region's food, its dialect word, its river or hill, the history of a neighbourhood, picked up from the people who live there, not from a lesson.
- **The map remembers:** places visited and clues found show on the city map and the country map, so the learner sees the country fill in.

## How it fits what's decided

- **Beginners:** the English carries the story and the explanations; the language is a few words worth keeping (*scavo*, *affresco*, *secolo*, *Guardi!*).
- **Missions** ([teach-plan.md](teach-plan.md) → Lessons and missions): an adventure or a mystery step can be a mission ("go back to the dig on Saturday", "ask the priest about the name in the register").
- **The syllabus:** experts and mysteries bring in the later topics naturally (the past, describing, opinions, reported speech).
- **The prompt knows the place:** the kind of place and why the person is there go into the prompt, so nobody gets an invented job.
- **Who's where** (correcting the redo's rule, which Claude wrote, not Josh): at a real business the person is staff with an invented name, never the real owner; at an invented business anyone fits, the owner included; customers and passers-by belong in public places. Josh's own rule was only "real businesses under their real names".
- **Open-ended world** (CLAUDE.md): adventures and mysteries are generated during play like everything else; what's hand-written is at most the shape (the kinds of places, people and mystery steps), as with the city pantries.
