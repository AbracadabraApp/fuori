# Vision

## One line

Learn Italian by spending a season in Italy as someone who belongs there, not as a tourist passing through.

## Who the learner is

The player is an **amante dell'Italia**: someone who loves Italy, has probably been before, and wants more than ordering off a menu. They want to be recognised at the bar, chat with the woman at the market about her tomatoes, argue gently about football, understand the jokes.

That changes the game in concrete ways:

| A tourist game would… | Fuori does… |
|---|---|
| Teach survival phrases for one-off transactions | Builds relationships with people you see again and again |
| Treat every place as a checklist stop | Lets you *stay* somewhere long enough to have a routine and a local bar |
| Use "Excuse me, where is the museum?" | Uses "Ciao Giulia, il solito?" by day three |
| Keep characters as service staff | Gives characters lives, opinions and curiosity about you |
| End when the transaction is done | Ends when the conversation naturally ends |

Characters should treat the player as a welcome newcomer who is making an effort, and warm up as the player returns. The player's own backstory (why they are in Italy, what they love about it) is set at the start and characters can ask about it.

## Level

Built around the creator's own level (A1–A2 by the book), but the real requirement is an attitude, not a level: **tolerance for not understanding** (decided 2026-10-11).

- **Immersion, not full understanding.** Understanding 20% of what the barista says and still getting your coffee and a recommendation is what being in Italy is like, and it's the experience. Characters speaking above your level is a feature, not a bug.
- **Each conversation adds a little.** The goal isn't to explain everything that went by; it's to catch the two or three things most worth having (see the review log in [Roadmap → M1](06-roadmap.md#m1-navigate-cities-and-hold-a-conversation-thats-remembered)).
- **Not a beginner course.** A Duolingo-style path (five-word sentences, drills, reviews) would be a different product. If Fuori is ever productised for true beginners, that's a separate decision.

The engine scales up by following CEFR levels, not by rewriting content (see [Levelling up](02-game-design.md#levelling-up)).

## Principles

1. **Voice first.** You speak, they speak. Typing is a fallback, not the main path.
2. **Forgive, then correct.** Bad pronunciation, transcription noise and small slips are forgiven. Real errors are corrected *in character*, by recasting ("Un cappuccino? Certo!"), with an optional quiet note on the side. Never a lecture, never a red X.
3. **People remember you.** Everyone you meet is saved and remembers your name, what you ordered, what you talked about. This is the main reason to come back.
4. **Real Italy.** Real places, real customs (standing at the bar, paying at the cassa first, the passeggiata), regional colour where it helps. No postcard clichés.
5. **Open world, never lost.** Go almost anywhere and meet whoever Claude creates; nothing is scripted. Every day still comes with a few suggestions, including a change of scenery, so a beginner always has an easy next step.
6. **Short sessions.** One in-game day should take 15–25 minutes.
7. **One teacher, everyone else a local.** Magda, the tutor, explains and drills. Everyone else talks to you like a person. A local may help the way kind Italians do with a newcomer ("si dice *il* caffè, eh", slowing down, repeating a word), but never steps out of character to lecture.

## What success looks like

- After a week of play, the learner can handle a bar, a market, a station and a short chat with a stranger without freezing.
- They choose where to go each day, and want to see what's next.
- They look forward to seeing specific characters again.
- Their diario shows recurring mistakes shrinking over time.
